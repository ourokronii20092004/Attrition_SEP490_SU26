# Ch06 · The Ward

The stair came down into warmth, and the warmth was the first lie.

He felt it before he saw anything — actual heat, real heat, rising up the stairwell to meet him as he descended, the first warmth in Eldravir that he had not made himself out of his own borrowed light. And after the parade ground, after the cistern, after a day and more of being the only fire in a frozen world and guarding that fire with his life, the warmth reached into him and *pulled*. His exhausted body leaned toward it before his mind had a vote — the way the dead leaned toward him, he realized with a lurch, the exact same helpless animal turning-toward, except that now he was the one doing the leaning, and the thing he leaned toward was comfort. He made himself notice it. That was the discipline he was building, rule by rule: notice the leaning, and distrust it precisely because it felt good. *The kindness is the trap.* He held the sentence up in front of him like the coin of light, small and steady, and went down into the warmth holding it.

The ward opened around him, and it was beautiful, and the beauty was worse than the clawed wall had been, because the wall had at least looked like what it was.

It had been a hospital, once — or a temple of healing, or whatever a world he'd never known had that was both at once. A long hall, vaulted in the same dark stone as everything else but lit, *lit*, not by the bruised light from above but by lamps, real warm amber lamps in brackets down the length of it, throwing gold up the columns and pooling soft on a floor that someone had kept clean. There were beds. Rows of them, low and white-sheeted and neat, made up with a care that caught at something in Ren's chest. And there was a smell — herbs, steeped in hot water, green and sharp and clean, and it cut through the sweet rot-smell that hung over the whole rest of the fragment so completely that for the first time since he woke he drew a breath that did not taste of something kept too long.

It was, in every particular his eye could find, a place of mercy. And Iris had told him it was a trap, and he stood at the foot of the stair and could not, looking at it, make the two facts sit together.

The dead here were not frozen in flight or fighting. That was the other difference, and it took him a moment to register it. Everywhere else in the city the dead had been caught in the worst second of their lives — reaching, fleeing, flinching, falling. Here they lay in the beds, tucked, their gray faces composed and smoothed and turned up toward the amber light, arranged with the tenderness you'd give a sleeping child. They looked, every one of them, *cared for.* And moving among them, down the long row, was a figure in healer's grays, small and round-shouldered, humming — the humming he'd heard from the stair, low and tuneless and entirely content, the sound of a person deep in work they love and have done so long that it no longer asks anything of the front of their mind.

---

She saw him, and her whole face lit, and she came down the row of beds with both hands already rising to reach for him.

She was small, and round, and gray like all the rest of them, but where the others had gone still and blank she was quick, bustling, alive with purpose — and her eyes were a warm wet brown, and they took him in head to foot in the space of a breath, and what came into them was not fear and not hunger but a kind of helpless delight, the delight of someone who has never once in her existence been anything but glad to see another person who might need her.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, *look* at you. Look at the state of you." Her hands were on him before he had decided whether to allow it — at his face, his jaw, turning his head toward the amber lamps, brisk and certain and unbearably gentle, the hands of a healer reading a patient in three practiced seconds. "Cold to the bone. And you've been spending yourself, I can see it, you've that gray look round the eyes — no, not gray like *us*, child, don't flinch, gray like a body that's burned more than it's had to burn. When did you last sleep? No — don't tell me, I can see you can't even remember, that's how far gone you've let yourself get. Sit. Sit *down*. I've a bed. I've a dozen beds, every one of them made up and waiting, sit yourself down."

"I—" Ren started, and found himself already being steered, with the irresistible competence of a person who has steered ten thousand reluctant patients into ten thousand beds, toward a low clean cot at the end of the nearest row.

"The sickness is in you already," she said, and her voice dropped, and the brightness in it thinned over something older and harder. "The bad air. The rot. I can feel it the way I feel a fever in a child — here," her fingertips touched his sternum, light as a moth, "and here," and his temple, the two exact places where the pressure had come down on him when the sky flinched in the cistern, "and it's early, it's so early, that's *good*, do you understand, that's very good, because early I can do so much. You came to me in *time*." She said the last word the way another person might say a prayer that had finally, against all hope, been answered. "They almost never come to me in time."

"Careful," Iris murmured, low, pitched for him alone, under the healer's bustle. "Every word she just said is true. That's the danger of this one — she isn't lying to you, and she isn't wrong. The sickness is in you, and it is early, and she really can ease it. Watch what the easing costs. Don't refuse it yet — you couldn't learn anything by refusing it blind. Let it begin. Just keep one cold eye open while it does, because the moment it starts you're going to want both eyes closed."

---

She eased it.

He let her, because he was tired in a way that had stopped being a feeling and become a fact of his body, a heaviness in the bones; and because Iris had said *let it begin*, and the only way to watch what the easing cost was to let the easing start.

She sat on the edge of the cot and took his hand in both of hers — her hands were warm, warm as the lamps, warm as no dead thing's hands had any right to be — and she closed her eyes and hummed, and something flowed out of her and into him through the contact. Cool, where the sickness ran hot. Still, where it churned. The low sick pressure behind his sternum eased and eased and let go. The grit behind his eyes washed clean. The deep burning ache in his arms, the drilling-ache he'd carried down from the parade ground, unknotted all at once — and behind it, behind all the particular hurts, a great drowsy painless ease came up through him like warm water filling a tub from the feet up, rising past his knees, his waist, his chest, and it was *wonderful*. It was the absence of everything that had been wrong since he opened his eyes under the bruised sky. It was the first time in his short remembered life that nothing hurt. His eyes started to close on their own, and he let them, and the warm water rose toward his chin.

"There," she breathed. "There it is. Let it. You don't have to hold anything in here — that's the whole trouble with all of you who come down to me, you hold and you hold, you've got it fixed in your heads that the holding is what keeps you, and it's the holding that's wearing you down to nothing. Here you can set it down. Here nobody hurts, and nobody's afraid, and nobody — *nobody*," and her gentle voice fierced suddenly on the word, gentle and ferocious at once, a vow, "nobody ever has to die. Not on my watch. Not one more. Sleep, child. I'll mind you. I'll mind you and the sickness can't have you and you can just rest, you can finally, finally rest."

And Ren almost did. He was a single breath from gone, the warm water at his chin, his whole body voting yes with everything it had.

Then his half-closed eyes, drifting, found the bed across the row, and the patient lying in it, and he came back up out of the ease cold and fast and entire.

The patient across the row was not dead.

Not gray. Not frost-furred. The patient across the row was a living woman — flushed, breathing, *warm*, another spark of true heat in the fragment, the second living thing he had seen since he woke — and she lay in Talwyn's clean white sheets with her eyes open and fixed on the amber lamps overhead and her face wiped utterly smooth. No fear in it. No pain. No thought. Nothing at all behind the open eyes but the ease — the same warm water he had been one breath from drowning in, except that she had drowned in it long ago, had been held under its surface so gently and so completely and for so long that there was nothing left of the person who had gone under. A living body, kept. A life held past the last point at which it was still a life, because the one thing the healer could not bear to do, the one thing in all the world she had set her whole soul against, was to let it end.

"There it is," Iris said softly. "Now you've seen the cost. There are a handful of the living still down here, Ren — real survivors, the last few of your world who didn't quite die when it died, who slipped the same edge you must have slipped. And Talwyn found them. And she has been *taking care of them.* For an age. Keeping them from every hurt — including, in the end, the hurt of being a person at all, because a person might sicken, a person might fail, a person might *die* — and she will burn the whole difference between living and not-living down to ash before she lets one more die on her watch." A pause. "That woman could have walked out, once. There's no one left home now to do the walking. Talwyn loved her to that. With her whole heart, every day, one gentle easing at a time. That is what her kindness is *for*, and that is what it *does*, and you, just now, were four breaths from the next bed down."

---

Ren took his hand back.

It was harder than stepping out of Karr's line had been. That had cost him fear; this cost him *comfort*, and he was learning, the deeper he went, that comfort was the more expensive of the two to give up. The instant his hand left hers the ease began to drain — the warm water sinking back down through him, knees, feet, gone — and the aches and the grit and the low sick pressure all came flooding back into their places, and his body cried out at the loss of it, honestly cried out, a wail of protest from every tired nerve. He nearly reached for her hand again just to make it stop. He didn't. And in the not-reaching he understood the rule he was about to make, before he had the words for it.

Talwyn's eyes had opened. The hurt in them was real and immediate — the specific hurt of a healer whose patient has pulled away from the cure, has *chosen* the sickness over her hands.

"You mustn't," she said. "Oh, you mustn't do that. I felt it ebb the moment you — child, the sickness doesn't *wait*, it doesn't pause to be polite, every hour you hold yourself apart from me it sinks deeper, and then one day it's too deep and even I can't reach it and you'll be—" Her voice caught on something. "Why would you take your hand back. I was helping. Helping is the only thing I do. It's the only thing I've ever done."

"What was their name," Ren said.

He didn't know where it came from. It came the way the report to Karr had come — out of the understanding, ahead of the planning of it, the right thing arriving in his mouth before he'd consciously assembled it. Talwyn went very still.

"Whose," she said.

"The first one you couldn't save." He kept his voice as gentle as he could and still ask it. "You said *not one more.* Not one *more.* That's not how a person talks who's only frightened of death in the general way. That's how a person talks who lost one in particular — one, before all the others — and stood up from it and swore. So. Who was it. The one this is all for. The one at the bottom of all these beds."

The ward was very quiet. Across the row, the living woman breathed her smooth, empty, untroubled breaths.

"My daughter," Talwyn said at last. The brightness was all gone from her now, and what lay under it was an age of grief worn so smooth by handling that it had no edges left to cut on — which was worse, somehow, than if it had been raw. "The first day. The very first day the sky came down — before I even understood we were at war, before anyone in the quarter did. She took a fever from the bad air. Just a fever. I was a healer, I was the best in the quarter, mothers brought me children other healers had given up on and I *brought them back* — and I held my own girl all that night and I did everything, everything I knew, everything there was, and at dawn she—" The gray hands folded in her lap, one over the other, very still. "I couldn't. I, who could save anyone, couldn't save the one that was mine. And I stood up from her little bed at that dawn and I decided. I decided that no one was ever going to feel what I felt standing up from that bed. Not on my watch. Not ever again. I would keep them. Every one of them, every soul that came to me, I would *keep*, and I would not let death take a single other—" She looked up at him, and the warm wet eyes were terrible now. "And I haven't. Do you understand what I'm telling you? In all this time. Not one more. Every soul that has come to me, I have kept. I *win*, child. Every single time. I never have to feel that dawn again."

"And the ones you're keeping," Ren said softly, his eyes going to the living woman across the row, the smooth untenanted face. "The ones still alive under all that minding. Are they winning too?"

Talwyn followed his gaze. And something moved across her face — some old, buried, long-refused knowing — and was smoothed away, fast, the way she smoothed everything, the way she'd smoothed a thousand faces flat. "She's *safe*," Talwyn said. "She'll never hurt again."

"That's not the same thing," Ren said. "Is it. And I think somewhere you know it isn't. I think that's why you couldn't look at her just now, when you said it."

"You should rest," Talwyn said, and stood, brisk again, the brightness coming back up over the grief like a lid set on a pot. "You're saying strange things, hard things, and that's the sickness — it gets into the thinking first, that's always how I know it's taken hold, it makes a person say cruel clever things to the one who's only trying to help them. Lie down. Lie down, child, and let me mind you, and in the morning none of this will frighten you anymore."

"That's the trap," Iris said quietly, in his ear. "Right there. *None of this will frighten you anymore.* She means it as the kindest thing she has to offer, and she's about to mean it more. But listen to it. It's word for word what the rot offers you — an end to being frightened. The healer's kindness and the corruption's whisper are about to become the same single voice, and when they do, a part of you that is so tired, Ren, is going to want very badly to say yes to it. I can't make that part of you be quiet. Only you can. So make the rule now, while you're cold and clear, because in the morning — fed, warmed, looked at like her own — you'll be neither."

Ren looked at the clean low cot, and the warm water waiting in it, and the smooth-faced living woman who would never hurt again and would never be anyone again, and he made the rule cold, while he still could.

*Don't take the painless thing.*

"Say it," Iris said.

"Don't take the painless thing." His fourth. He looked at Talwyn's bright, loving, terrible face. "Even when I want it. *Especially* when I want it."

"Good," Iris said. "Now the hard part — because making the rule while you're cold is the easy half. By morning she'll have understood what you are: a living warmth she could keep forever, the prize of all prizes, the one patient she could win and never, ever lose. And she'll stop asking and start *helping*. And you'll have to do for her what you did for Karr. Except Karr didn't know his truth, and the truth was what freed him — and she already knows hers, all of it, she's known it since that dawn. So a fact won't free her. It'll have to be something harder than a fact. Sleep if you can. Don't let her hold your hand while you do."

Ren lay down on the clean cot, in the amber light, in the ward where no one was allowed to die, and folded his hands on his own chest where hers couldn't reach them without his knowing, and did not sleep, and waited for the morning — and for the hardest kindness he had ever had to refuse, and then, somehow, find a way to give back.
