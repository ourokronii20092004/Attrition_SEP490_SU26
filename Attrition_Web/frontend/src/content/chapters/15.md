# Ch15 · Permission to Fail

"I'll tell you plainly," Iris said. "Because you've earned plain, and because — I find I don't want to do the other thing with you. The managing. The withholding. The handing-over of a true thing in three careful pieces so it lands where I've decided it should land. I've done that the whole way down, and I'm tired of it in a way I don't usually get tired of things, and I'd rather just say it. So. Plainly."

Ren waited, in the violet quiet, with Maren holding the door and a whole dead world hanging on what came next.

"When that seal falls," Iris said, "the fragment ends. Truly ends — the way it should have an age ago. The half-death finishes. The rot stops, because there is nothing left for it to rot. The whole of Eldravir lets go, all at once. The dead you've freed are already at peace; they'll simply be *gone*, finished, the way the living are meant to be finished, into the dark that's allowed at last to be only the dark. No rupture. No collision with my world. The thing I needed done gets done." A pause. "And it can't hurt me, and it can't reach the place I guard, and the danger that started the whole of this is over. That part is exactly what I told you in the first hour, under the bruised sky. I did not lie to you about the work."

"But," Ren said. He had grown used to the *buts* at the bottoms of things.

"But you are inside it," Iris said. "You are the one living thing in a fragment that is about to finish dying. And here is the part I did not tell you — the part I've been not-telling you since the first hour, and that I am telling you now because you are about to make a choice and you cannot, you *must* not, make it in the dark." Her voice had gone bare, stripped of every dry inflection he'd come to know it by. "There are three ways this ends, for you. And only one of them I would call a rescue, and even that one I am not certain is a kindness — and I am, for the first time in longer than your world has existed, genuinely uncertain which of them I want for you. Which is not a thing I have said, or felt, about anyone, in a very long time."

---

"Tell me the three," Ren said.

"One. You let the seal fall, and you don't get out in time, and the fragment takes you with it as it ends — or, worse, the rot in you wins first, here at the bottom where it's strongest, where your arm's already gone to the shoulder. You take the painless thing at last, because you're tired, and it's right there, and the choice in front of you is unbearable, and a man at the end of his strength reaches for the thing that makes the strength stop being needed. Either way the living boy becomes another piece of the ending. I lose you the way I lost the five. I take what I learned from your descent, and I grieve you in my fashion, and I handle the next dead world without you." Flat. Unsentimental. "That one is close, Ren. Closer than you'll want to hear. Your arm is dead to the shoulder and you've been spending yourself the whole way down. Don't tell yourself it isn't a live road. By the cold count, it's the most likely of the three."

"Two."

"Two. You let the seal fall, and you use the one thing you have that nothing else in here has — your mortality, your living, breakable ability to *end* — to break the loop clean, and you get yourself out before it closes. Back up the strata. Out through the square where you woke. Out of the fragment entirely, before it finishes dissolving behind you. You walk out alive. Under a real sky — a true one, the world I guard, not that bruise overhead. You carry Eldravir out in your memory, the way you promised Tomas, and you *live.* Your whole world is gone, finished, laid properly to rest at last — and you survived it, the last child of a people who get to be remembered now instead of rotting. It's bittersweet, and it's clean, and it is the closest thing to a happy ending this was ever going to have." A pause. "I would take it, Ren. If I were you. I want to be honest that I would take it, and not think less of myself, and not think less of you. Walk out. Live. You have more than earned a quiet life under a real sky."

"And three," Ren said, "is the one you actually want. I can hear it. You've been circling it since the Archive."

The violet quiet held.

"Three," Iris said, "is the truth I've been not-telling you since the bottom of Tomas's stair. You asked him to let you *carry* his world out, instead of keeping it stopped on his shelves. *Carry them, don't keep them.* Do you understand what you were describing, when you said that to him? You weren't only describing a kindness to a frightened clerk." A pause, and the bare voice went barer. "You were describing *me.*"

---

"I keep what's ending," Iris said. "I told you that in the first hour, and it was true, and you heard it the way anyone would — a god's tidy hobby. A collector of dead worlds. Detached, cool, filing the universe's losses on some vast cold shelf of her own. It isn't that. Listen to what it actually is, because you're the first I've ever told, and I find I want one person in the worlds to have heard it said plainly before I decide whether to ask the thing I'm about to ask."

The violet hung still around them. Maren held her door. The dead world waited.

"There are worlds dying all the time," Iris said. "Everywhere. More than you could count, more than I can reach, ending in the dark between things — and most of them die the way yours so nearly did. Dissolved into the Void. Unmade into nothing. Every soul, every song, every small kept moment of every life that was ever lived in them, gone, as though it had never been at all. Not remembered. Not mourned. Not even *known* to have existed, by anyone, anywhere, ever again. Uncounted." And he heard it then, suddenly, with a lurch — heard exactly why she had understood Tomas so completely, why she'd lingered at his shelves, why her voice had done the bare thing there. "I cannot save them. Understand that first. A god of a dead world is still only one thing, and I cannot be *inside* the fragments — I shatter them, I'm too heavy, you've known that since the first hour. So I cannot go down into a dying world and give it an ending, the way you've given this one its ending, layer by layer. I can only stand at the breach, and keep the Void off long enough for the dying to finish, and *witness* it — hold the memory of a whole world in myself, so that it ends *remembered* instead of erased. That is what I am, Ren. Not a keeper of endings the way a jailer keeps a cell. I am the thing that makes certain a dying world is *carried* — out of the dark, in someone — instead of merely lost. I am the only memory a dissolved world ever gets."

"And it isn't enough," Ren said. Quietly. He had understood it already, in the shape of her voice.

"It has never been enough," Iris said. "Because I witness from the *breach.* From outside. I can hold the shape of a world — its size, its name, the fact of it, the date of its death. The obituary. But I can never go in, and so I never get the *truth* of it from within — never the thing you've spent this descent learning, the Karr of it, the Talwyn of it, the way Maren's hands tightened when she spoke of the wall, the real texture of the people who lived and died in it. I get the fact that a world existed. I never get the world. Every fragment I've kept, across an age longer than I'll tell you, I've kept only from the outside — the cover of a book I was never able to open." A long pause, and the bare voice went all the way bare, down to the floor of itself. "And then I felt a living child, sleeping at the bottom of his own people's grave. And I understood what he might become, if he could be woken, and pointed down, and survived the going. Someone who could do what I never can. Walk *into* a dying world, and understand it, all the way to the bottom, person by person — and give it a real ending — and carry the truth of it out, alive, in a living mind that goes on living. Not the obituary. The world itself, carried."

The pieces came together in Ren with a click that was almost physical, almost a sound.

"That's what I am to you," he said. "Not just a living soul who can walk the stasis without breaking it. You've sent those, and lost them. You need a living soul who can go *in*, where you can't, and bring out the truth of the thing. The part you can never reach from the breach. You don't need a key that opens one door. You need—"

"I need *hands,*" Iris said. "I have never had hands. An age of standing at the breach, keeping the dark off the dying, holding their obituaries — and never once able to reach inside and carry one out whole. You can. You did. You walked into a dead world and you understood it all the way down and you're about to carry it out alive, every soul of it, in the one thing that can actually hold a person — and I have watched you do it, layer by layer, and I have thought, since before I woke you: *there. There is the thing I have been missing for an age. There are the hands.*" A pause, and the bare voice caught on something, the only time it ever had. "And before you answer, you have to hear the worst of it, because it's standing at the edge of the light and you've already paid for my silence once today. The thing that took your boy. The fifth. I told you it was the best of them — the one I was proudest to send, the one most like you. That was the truest thing I've said down here and the one I most wish weren't true. *They were the closest I had ever come.* Further down than any of the others, sharper, surer — I watched them go stratum by stratum and I let myself believe, the way I let myself believe about you, that this one would reach the bottom and carry a world out and I would finally have my hands. And then, at the deep of it, tired past telling, they took the painless thing — not by inches, the way the gentle ones fade, but all at once and entire, because the ones who go furthest have the most self to surrender and surrender it hardest. And there was nothing left to file. Only hunger, with their face on it. I could not carry them out. I could not give them an ending. They are the one thing in any world I have failed so completely that the failure walks. So when I tell you the work is a life sentence to other people's endings — I am also telling you it is a life sentence to your own failures, and that they do not always stay buried, and that the worst of them learns your face. I sent you down the same stair that made *that.* Hold that, too, while you choose."

A silence. Then, gently: "That's the third way out, Ren. You don't walk out and live a quiet life under a real sky. You walk out, and you take up the work. My work. The thing I've done alone at the breach forever — except now it has hands that can reach inside. You become the one who goes into the dying worlds and carries them out from within. The next one, and the next, and the next. And I will not dress it up for you, because I learned from watching you that the dressing-up is a slower cruelty. It is the most important thing I can imagine anyone doing. And it is a life sentence to other people's endings — an eternity of grief that is never your own, world after world after world, each one a descent like this one, each one a Karr and a Talwyn and a Maren of its own, and now and then a thing at the edge of the light that used to be someone you sent. I do not know whether offering it to you is the kindest or the cruelest thing I have ever done. I have done some cruel things. *She is mysterious* — that's the thing they say about me, isn't it, where I'm from. I am being the precise opposite of mysterious right now. I am telling you exactly what I am, and exactly what I want, and exactly what it would cost you, down to the floor. *Carry them, don't keep them.* I learned the words from you, in Tomas's archive. They are the truest thing I know, and I have never once in an age been able to do the thing they describe — from the inside. You can. That is the whole of it. That is what you are to me."

---

Ren sat at the bottom of his dead world, alive, with the whole carried company of it inside him and the last of his people holding a door one breath from rest, and a god's true face turned toward him at last — three doors in front of him: to end, to escape, or to inherit the loneliest and most necessary work in all the worlds.

"How much of this did you arrange," he said quietly. "The descent. The order of them. The rules I made. Did you walk me to this — was I always going to end up here, at the bottom, deciding this, because you built the road that way?"

"I don't know," Iris said.

And it was the first time, in the whole of the descent, that she had said those three words to him without a plan hidden somewhere behind them.

"Truly. I don't know. I woke you, and I pointed you down, and I held the things off your back, and I answered what I felt like answering and withheld what I felt like withholding. But the rules were *yours.* I didn't give you a single one of them — I'd never have found *don't take the painless thing*, I'd have made the cold call in Talwyn's ward and counted the cost and moved on. The third things were yours. The pulpit was yours; I'd have let the lie stand or torn it down, and either way I'd have broken them. The report to Karr was yours. I chose you for what you were, and I hoped — I *hoped*, which is not a thing I do often — for what you might become. But I did not script one word you said to any of them, because the entire point, the whole reason it had to be you and could not be me, is that I *can't.* I can't find the third thing. I've never been able to. So — did I engineer you to this choice? I engineered you to the *doorway*, Ren. I built the road that brought you to the threshold. What you do standing in it is the one thing in all of this that I was never able to arrange, and never will be, with you or with anyone. That isn't me being mysterious. It's just the truth. And it's why the choice is real. And it's why it's yours."

Behind Ren, Maren held the seal, and waited.

Above him, an age of freed dead had made their peace, and waited.

Outside, past the bruised lid of the sky, in the dark between the worlds, a thing the size of a city drifted toward a god's world and did not yet turn to look — and the clock she would never name ran on.

And Ren understood that the next thing he did would be the thing the whole descent had been walking him toward from the first cold breath — not a fact to deliver, not a lie to refuse, but the one rule he could only make by *living* it: what do you do, when you finally understand everything, with a world that won't die, and a god who can't grieve it, and a life of your own that you've only just, barely, gotten back.

"Let me think," he said. "You said there's no clock down here but the one outside. Give me until I know."

"Take it," Iris said softly. "I'll hold the door with Maren. Take all the time the world will give you, down to the last of it. And Ren—" the bare voice, one more time, "—whatever you choose. You already did the thing I most needed done. You read it. You ended it true. The rest is yours to keep, or to carry, or to set down and walk away from — and I will not think less of you for any of the three. I'm not certain I'm able to think less of you anymore. That, too, is new."

Ren closed his eyes in the violet quiet, at the bottom of the world he had been born in and slept through the death of, and began to decide.
