# Ch09 · No One Is Coming

He sat in the cathedral through the long candle-hours and let the song wheel round and round, and somewhere in the middle of it he found the third thing — not by thinking harder at it, but by stopping thinking entirely, and simply listening to what the dead were actually singing underneath the words.

Because they were not singing about rescue. He had thought they were. *They are coming, hold fast and they will come* — he had heard it as a claim about the future, a prediction, the lie Bran kept them in. And on the surface it was exactly that. But he sat among them long enough, through enough turns of the song, to hear the thing beneath the claim — and the thing beneath was not *they will come.* The thing beneath was older and truer and had nothing to do with any gate. The thing beneath was: *we held fast together, and it was not nothing, and we did not face the dark alone.*

The hope had never really been about the rescue. The hope was about each other. About being people who, in the worst hour any of them would ever know, had chosen to stand up and believe and hold one another's hands rather than break apart and die screaming and separate. The rescue was only the story they told so that they could do that — the shape they poured the holding-together into so it had somewhere to go. The promised dawn was the excuse. The hands clasped in the dark were the truth the excuse had been built to protect.

And once he heard that — once he could hear the clasped hands under the promised dawn — he knew, exactly, what he could take from them and what he could not.

"I think I've got it," he said quietly. "The third thing."

"Tell me," Iris said, "before you do it. Once. Out loud. So that I know you've actually found it, and aren't simply tired enough, after a night of that singing, to break something beautiful and call the breaking honesty."

---

"The lie has to go," Ren said. "I can't leave it. Not because true matters more than kind — you were right to throw that at me, and I sat with it all night, and it's a real question and I'm not pretending it isn't. But because the lie is *costing* them the very thing it pretends to protect. They're not at peace down here. They're suspended. Every one of them is frozen one hour short of a truth, and the freezing is the harm — it's the same harm as Karr's drilling, the same as Talwyn's keeping, exactly the same, just wearing the prettiest mask of the three. A hope that's never allowed to resolve into anything isn't a comfort. It's a cage with a beautiful view painted on the inside of the bars." He paused. "So, no. I won't preserve it. *Don't lie to be kind* — that's the rule, that's the fifth one, I can feel it setting as I say it. A kindness built on a lie isn't kindness. It's a slower cruelty with better manners, and it costs the people you're being kind to the chance to ever be real again."

"And the cruelty of taking it? The terror you drop them into? You watched them all night. You know exactly what it'll look like when their faces change."

"That's the part I had wrong," Ren said. "All night I thought the choice was: leave them happy in the lie, or make them understand they died waiting for nothing. Two doors, both terrible. But that's not the only way to end a lie. You can tear it down — *no one came, you were fools, it was all for nothing* — and yes, that drops them straight into the terror they were spared, and it's a cruelty dressed up as honesty. *Or* you can end it by handing them back the *true* thing the lie was built to protect. Tell them no help is coming — and in the same breath, in the very same breath, tell them why their hope was never foolish. Because it was never really about the gate." He looked out over the radiant rows. "They didn't stay happy because they believed a rescue was on the road. They stayed *human* because they held each other in the dark instead of breaking apart. The rescue was just the story that let them do it. I can take the story and leave the thing the story was protecting. I can tell them the truth and tell them they were right — both at once. That's the third thing. You take the false hope and you leave the true one standing."

The cathedral sang on, oblivious, gold and certain. Iris was quiet for a moment, and the quiet had a weight to it.

"That's it," she said at last, and there was something almost unsteady under the evenness, something he'd never heard from her. "That's the third thing. I have watched people break that lie in this cathedral for an age, and every one of them broke the people along with it, because every one of them believed that honesty meant taking everything — that to be true you had to be total. You found the part of the hope that was *true*, and you're going to hand it back to them as you take the false part away." A pause. "Go on, then. Climb the pulpit. I'll be quiet now. This one is yours, all the way."

---

He climbed the pulpit — Bran's pulpit, the high place from which the keeper of candles had once spoken his one true miracle, and from which he had spoken an age of true-feeling lies since. And Bran let him climb it, watching with his pale ecstatic eyes, certain even now, certain to the last, that whatever the warm stranger meant to say from up there would be one more glad voice added to the great glad chorus of the waiting.

The song faltered as the dead saw a living man stand where their priest stood. The radiant gray faces turned up to him, row on row, lit gold from below. For the first time since he had come down the stair, he had their attention — all of it — and he understood that it was the single most fragile thing he would ever hold in his hands, and he did not let his voice shake.

"I've come from the gate," he said. The same words he'd given Karr, because they were true, and because the form of them mattered — a man arriving with news. "The eastern gate. The one your priest was shown, long ago — the one that opened, when help truly came through it and saved hundreds of you who would otherwise have died in those three days." He let it land. He let them have the memory, whole and warm and real, before he touched it. "That was real. I need you to hear that first, before anything else I say. The night your priest was shown the gate, and rose, and spoke it — and it came true, three days later, exactly as he saw — that was real. It happened. It was the truest miracle this city ever had, and it saved hundreds of lives, and he was *right.* Hold onto that. Whatever else I tell you, keep that. He was right, and it was real, and it mattered."

The congregation glowed up at him. Bran, below the pulpit, lifted his burned face, and it was full of vindication, of *see, see, the stranger has come to confirm it.*

"But it only happened once," Ren said. Gently. And he felt the great room begin, by degrees, to still. "And it is not going to happen again. There's no relief column on the road. There's no garrison left standing to send one. There's no dawn coming over any mountain — because the world that would have sent the help, the world the help would have come *from*, died. All of it. The same night you did. You have been waiting an age for a gate that is never going to open again, because there is no longer anyone alive on the other side of it to walk through. No one is coming." He watched the words go out over the rows, watched the radiance begin to crack along its edges, faces beginning to change — and he did not stop, because to stop now, to leave them half in the truth, suspended between the lie and the whole of it, would be the real cruelty. "No one is coming. I am so sorry. I know it is the last thing in any world you wanted to hear, and I know your priest kept it from you out of love, real love, for an age — and I am telling you anyway, because you deserve to be people who know the truth more than you deserve to be kept happy not knowing it. You deserve to be *real* more than you deserve to be comfortable. Even now. Even this late."

A sound moved through the cathedral — the first stirring of the terror Iris had warned him of, an age of held-off grief shifting in a thousand radiant throats, beginning to wake.

And Ren spoke over it, into it, faster now, the way he'd spoken to Talwyn as she went under.

"But *listen* — listen, because here is the other true thing, and it matters more than the gate ever did, it matters more than anything I've said." He gripped the rail of the pulpit. "You think you stayed happy all this time because you believed the rescue was coming. You didn't. I sat with you all night and I listened to what you were really singing, underneath the words — and it was never the gate. You stayed *human* because you held each other. Because when the sky broke and the fear came down, every one of you chose — chose, in the worst hour you would ever know — to stand together and hold fast and clasp the next person's hand rather than break apart and die alone and screaming in the dark. The rescue was only the story you told so that you could do that. And the story was a lie. But the holding was *real.* And the holding is the thing that actually saved you — not from dying, no one could be saved from that, not you, not anyone, in any world — but from dying *afraid, and alone*, which is the only rescue that was ever truly on offer to anyone, anywhere, ever." His voice was breaking now and he let it break, let them hear it break. "No one came. And you were *never fools* to hope — because the hope was how you held each other's hands through the dark. You did the bravest thing that people can do. You were right. You can stop waiting now — not because you failed, but because you already did the thing that mattered, an age ago, and no one ever came to tell you that you could stop. So I'm telling you. It's done. You held fast. You can let go."

The cathedral was utterly silent.

And then, in the front row, an old gray woman who had been singing since before Ren was born lowered her face slowly into her hands — and not in terror. In something else. In the enormous, bottomless relief of a person finally, *finally* permitted to set down a hope she had been carrying so long that it had grown heavier than any despair. And beside her, another. And beyond that, another, and another, down the rows — not breaking. *Resting.* An age of suspended joy resolving, at last, into the grief it had been built to hold off — and the grief, felt at last, did not destroy them. It completed them. It was the thing that let them, finally, go.

---

Bran stood at the foot of his own pulpit and watched his congregation lay down the hope he had guarded for an age, and his burned thin face was a war.

"You've unmade them," he whispered. "I held them in the light, and you've — they're weeping, brother, they were *happy*, they were the only happy thing left in this whole dead place, and you've—"

"They're grieving," Ren said, coming down the pulpit steps to him. "It's not the same as unmaking. You kept them from grieving for an age because grief *looks* like breaking — it has the same shape from the outside, the bent head, the covered face. But look closer. Look at them, Bran, really look. They're not breaking. They're finishing. They're doing the thing the grief was always for, the thing you wouldn't let them do because you couldn't bear to watch it and couldn't bear to do it yourself." He met the pale stricken eyes. "You did a true thing once. You were shown the gate, and you spoke it, and it saved hundreds. That was real, and it will always have been real, and nothing I've said tonight touches it. But you spent everything after it trying to *be* the man that one miracle made you — the one who's always right, the one whose hope always comes true — and you couldn't be him, because no one can, because the gate only opens once and then the world goes on dying anyway. You weren't wrong to hope. You were only wrong to *promise.* And you can stop now. You don't have to keep being right. You just have to let them go, and go with them."

"But if the rescue was a lie," Bran said, and his voice had gone small, a child's voice, the keeper of candles who had been no one, "then what was I? If I was wrong all this time — every day, every song — then the one true night, the gate, was that — was even *that* — how do I know even that was—"

"That one was true," Ren said, firmly, taking the thin gray shoulders. "Don't you let go of that one. That one was real. Let go of all the times after, when you tried to make it true again by saying it louder. Keep the night God showed you the gate. Keep the night you were no one, and you rose, and you spoke a true thing, and hundreds lived because of it. That was you at your best — that was the whole reason you mattered, and it stays. It was never the lying that made you matter. It was the one true word, spoken when it counted." He felt the burned shoulders shake under his hands. "You were a good priest, Bran. Once for real, and then for an age out of love, which is its own kind of true even when the words aren't. That's not nothing. That's almost everything a person gets to be. You can rest now."

And the Flame — who had burned an age to keep one beautiful lie alight, because it had been true the single night that made him — let the fire gutter down. The fever went out of the pale eyes, and for one last moment they found something quieter and truer than ecstasy, which was peace. He turned from Ren. He went to his congregation. He knelt down *among* them, no longer above them on any pulpit, just one more of his own flock at last, and he wept with them — and the candlelight eased, all down the great vaulted dark, ten thousand flames sinking gentle and gold, and the cathedral of the joyful dead became, at last, simply a place where the dead were allowed to be done.

Ren stood in the failing gold and found that he was weeping too, and that his hands were shaking, and that the fifth rule was driven down into him now as deep as any of the others.

"*Don't lie to be kind,*" Iris said, softly. "Five rules. You spoke the worst truth there is — *no one is coming* — and you found the one way to say it that set them free instead of shattering them. Do you understand what you did just now, Ren? You took the false hope and you left the true one standing. That distinction—" she paused, and when she went on her voice had the careful, deliberate weight of someone laying down a thing that would matter later, "—that distinction is the whole of it. It's the thing at the very bottom of this place, the thing the last of them is sitting on. Hold onto it all the way down. Because when you reach the throne, the question is going to be exactly that one, scaled up to the size of an entire world: which hope was false, and which was true, and whether the difference is worth what it will cost you to act on it."

The candles dimmed to embers. Behind the altar, under the great dark arch, a low door stood open on a stair going down, and from it came no song and no warmth — only a vast, dry, continuous rustling, like a wind moving through endless paper, like a thing turning pages without end and without rest.

"The fourth," Iris said. "His name is Tomas. He was the Archive — the Memory of this world. He kept everything, because he could not bear for anything to be forgotten." A pause, and her voice tightened by a degree he had learned to read as significant. "And, Ren — in there, among the things he kept, you are going to find the people who came before you. The ones I sent. The ones the Void took. He filed them. They're down there, replaying, every one. I'm telling you now, while you're braced and clear-headed, so that it doesn't take you in the dark and unready. You're going to meet my failures. And you're going to learn what happened to them, and it is going to make the deal you and I struck in that first hour look very different than it looked when you took it." A long pause. "I would rather you knew that going down the stair than discovered it at the bottom. I am not in the habit of warning people before the hard part. Consider it a measure of how far I've revised my estimate of you."

Ren wiped his face with the back of his hand. He looked at the low dark door, and the dry endless rustle beyond it.

And he noticed — he made himself notice it, because *don't lie to yourself* applied hardest to the things about himself he'd rather not see — that he was not, walking toward a fourth stratum and the failures of the five who came before him, afraid. He was *confident.* Three pillars down, three for three, each one a knot no one else had untied and he had. The count Iris kept had become a count he kept, and it had grown into something warmer and more dangerous than a count: a sense of his own hand, his own eye, his own gift for finding the door in the wall that everyone else had missed. *The ones who came before me failed here. I won't.* He thought it almost without words, and there was no cruelty in it and no arrogance he'd have recognized as arrogance — only the quiet certainty of a man who has done a hard thing three times and has stopped, somewhere, believing he can do it wrong. He did not hear the danger in the thought. The whole trouble with that particular confidence is that it is indistinguishable, from the inside, from simply being good at the thing — and he was good at the thing, which made it worse, because it meant the evidence all agreed with him right up until the moment it didn't.

And from below the low dark door, under the dry rustle of the endless pages, he heard it once more — the single tread, the deliberate one, nearer now than it had been at Karr's gate, no longer somewhere vaguely down the descent but *close*, a stratum or two at most, even and unhurried and waiting. He thought, with the new confidence: *whatever it is, I'll understand it too.* He did not say it to Iris. He had stopped, a little, asking Iris things he felt he already had the measure of.

"Spend small," he said, counting the rungs of the ladder he'd built in the dark. "Don't lie to myself. Don't freeze. Don't take the painless thing. Don't lie to be kind." Five, now. A railing he could hold in the dark going down. "All right."

He went down into the Archive.
