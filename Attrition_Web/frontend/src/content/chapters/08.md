# Ch08 · The Cathedral

The stair came down into light, and song, and the smell of flowers that had no business existing in a dead world, and Ren stood at the bottom of it and understood within ten seconds why Iris had braced him.

It was a cathedral. Deep now — they had come a long way down, and there was no broken roof here, no shaft of bruised sky; they were under stone, in the foundations of faith. And the space was lit, but not by lamps and not by the violet from above. It was lit by candles. Thousands of them. Real flame, the first honest fire he had seen since the parade ground — banked along every ledge and rail and altar-step, climbing the dark columns in tiers, throwing a warm and living gold up into a vaulted dark that should have swallowed it and somehow did not. And the air was sweet — not the kept-too-long sweetness that hung over the rest of the fragment, the corpse-sweet he'd learned to breathe past, but actual sweetness, flowers, incense, the smell of a place prepared for something joyful, a wedding, a homecoming, a feast.

And it was full.

Not of the frozen, caught mid-flight. Not of the kept, smoothed flat in their beds. Of the *gathered.* The dead of this stratum sat in their rows and stood in their aisles and knelt at their rails, and they were all of them turned the same way, toward a high altar at the far end under a great dark arch, and they were singing — and their gray faces were lifted, and lit gold from below by the candles, and *radiant.* Happy. Filled with a happiness so complete and so unguarded that Ren's throat closed on the sight of it, because he had not seen happiness since he woke, had not known until this moment how much he'd missed the simple sight of it on a face, and here was a whole congregation of it, gray and dead and shining.

They were not suffering. That was the wrongness, and it took him a moment to locate it, because it was the absence of the thing he'd grown used to. Everywhere else in Eldravir the dead had been caught in the worst second of their lives. Here they had been caught in a moment of pure joy — and held there — and the holding had not curdled it the way it had curdled everything else. They were happy. They had been happy for an age. And they were singing about the thing that made them happy, the same few lines coming round and round, and Ren caught the words as the song wheeled past again, and wished, immediately and completely, that he hadn't.

*They are coming, they are coming, hold fast and they will come; the dawn is on the mountain and the help is on the road.*

---

"No help came," Ren said. It was not a question. He already knew the shape of the answer; he'd learned the shape of this place.

"No help came," Iris agreed. "There was no one left to send it. The world was dying everywhere at once — there was no untouched quarter with troops to spare, no relief column forming in some safe province, no dawn on any mountain anywhere. These people died waiting for a rescue that did not exist and was never going to exist. And Bran held them in the waiting — kept them in the last hour, the hour before they would have understood, the hour when they still believed and the believing still made the dying bearable. Because he could not stand to let them feel the rest of it. Look at them, Ren. Look how happy. That is the single cruelest mercy in this entire grave, and it is the one you came down here to undo."

Ren walked up the central aisle, between the radiant singing rows, and — this was the other difference, and it unsettled him as much as the joy — none of them turned to lean at his warmth. They were too full of their gladness to notice him. He was a cold draft passing unremarked through a wedding feast. He reached the altar steps, and the figure standing above them turned to him, and that was Bran.

He was tall, and thin, and burned-looking — a man made of zeal and very little flesh, in the robes of a priest gone gray like all the rest of them. But his eyes, like Karr's, like Talwyn's, had kept their color, and his were a pale ecstatic blue, and they fixed on Ren with the radiant unshakeable certainty of a man who has never once, in life or in death, doubted anything.

"Brother," Bran said, and his voice was warm and ringing and glad, a voice built for a pulpit. "You've come down out of the dark above. You look so tired, brother — so afraid. But you needn't be. Not here. Not anymore. Here, we *know.* Sit with us. Sing with us. The rescue is coming — it has been promised, it was shown to me with my own eyes, and any hour now the great doors will open and the light will come down and all this waiting will be over, and we will be lifted up out of the dark together, every one of us, into the morning. Stay. Wait with us. The waiting is almost done. It's always almost done."

"Shown to you," Ren said. "With your own eyes. What were you shown?"

And Bran's burned face lit from within with the memory of it, and he told it — and the terrible thing, the thing that landed in Ren like a stone dropped into still water, was that it had the absolute, unmistakable ring of truth.

---

"In the first days," Bran said, "when the sky first broke, and the fear came down on the city like a fever in the blood, I prayed. I was no one then — a minor priest, a keeper of candles in a side chapel, a man whose name no one had any reason to know. And in the night, as I prayed, I was *shown* a thing. Not a feeling, brother — not a hope, not a wish dressed up as a vision. *Shown.* The way you are shown the sun when someone turns your face to the window. I saw the eastern gate. I saw it open. I saw a light come through it that was not the bad light of the broken sky, and I saw our people walk out of the dark through that gate, and live. I saw it whole and certain. And I rose from my knees with my heart on fire, and I climbed up to the great pulpit — me, the keeper of candles, no one — and I told the whole terrified city: *hold fast. I have seen the eastern gate open. Help is coming. Hold fast.*"

The candles trembled. His pale eyes shone.

"And it *came true.*" His voice dropped to a kind of holy hush. "Three days later, brother. Three days. The eastern gate opened, and a relief came through it — soldiers, grain, water, hope — sent from a garrison we'd thought already fallen, and hundreds who would have died in those three days lived, because they held fast on the strength of what I had been shown. It was real. I was *right.* God put a true thing into my eyes and I spoke it aloud and the speaking of it kept hundreds alive. Do you see now? Do you understand what I am? I am not a man telling pretty lies in the dark to frighten children into calm. I am a man who was shown, and proven. And so when I tell them now — hold fast, the rescue is coming — I am not hoping. I am telling them what I *know*, what I have always known, what was true once and so must be true again. The doubt is the only sin there is. I held the doubt off them. I kept them in the faith, and the faith kept them happy, and—" He gestured out at the radiant singing rows, his life's work, his whole congregation held forever one hour short of the truth. "And look. Look how they wait. Not one of them afraid. Not in an age. I gave them that. And I will go on giving it to them until the doors open."

"There it is," Iris said quietly, in his ear. "Now you understand him. And now you understand why he is the cruelest of all of them. Karr's hope was empty, and he couldn't see that it was. But Bran's hope was *real* — once. He had a true miracle. That is the hook he is caught on, and it is barbed in a way the others' weren't. He cannot let go of the lie, because the lie was true the one time that mattered most, and to call it a lie now is to unmake the single moment his entire self is built on — the night God showed him the gate, and he was right, and his rightness saved hundreds." A pause. "And here is your trap, Ren, and it's the reverse of every one you've faced. These people are *happy.* They died in joy and they have waited in joy for an age and not one of them has ever, even for an hour, been afraid. If you do to Bran what you did to Karr — if you stand up and tell the truth, *no one is coming, no one was ever coming, the gate will not open again* — you do not free them into peace. You free them into the terror they were spared. You take a whole cathedral of the radiant and you make them understand, all at once, that they died waiting for nothing, that the singing was for nothing, that the hope was a thing their priest spun to keep them quiet while they waited to be dissolved. Is that mercy? Or are you only a cold man who cannot bear the sight of the dead smiling — who'd break the one beautiful thing left in this entire grave because *true* matters more to him than *kind*?"

Ren stood at the foot of the altar, among the singing, in the trembling gold of ten thousand candles, and found that he did not have the answer.

His rules had carried him through Karr and through Talwyn. Here, for the first time, they fractured against each other. *Don't lie to yourself* said plainly that the rescue was never coming and was not coming now. But the rule he did not yet have — the one this stratum existed to teach him — was about whether a truth that could only wound was a truth he had any right to inflict on people who had done nothing but hold each other in the dark.

"I don't know what to do," he said. And saying it cost him something, and he understood, even as it cost him, that the saying was itself a kind of refusing-to-lie — that *I don't know* was truer, right now, than any confident thing he could have reached for.

"Good," Iris said, and she did not say it unkindly. "That is the right place to begin, with this one. The people who came down here certain — certain they must preserve the lie, or certain they must shatter it — every one of them got it wrong, and got it wrong in both directions, and left this cathedral worse than they found it. You're going to have to find the third thing. The way you found it for Karr. So sit with them a while. Sing, if you can stand to. Watch what the joy actually is, underneath the words of it. And in the morning you'll have to choose — and I am not going to choose for you, because this, exactly this, is the kind of choice I sent a living man down here to make instead of me. I deal in the larger ledger. I'd have made the cold call long ago and counted the cost and moved on. You might find something better than the cold call. That is the entire reason you're here. That is, perhaps, the whole reason I—"

She stopped.

It was the first time, in the whole descent, that he had heard her stop herself.

"Rest," she said instead, after a moment. "Tomorrow is the hardest thing you've done."

Ren sat down in the back row of the cathedral of the joyful dead, among the radiant gray faces lifted toward a rescue that would never come, and listened to them sing about the dawn on the mountain, and did not yet know whether the kindest thing he could possibly do was to let them keep singing forever, or to climb that pulpit and tell them the dawn was a lie — and he understood, sitting there, that *this*, not Karr, not Talwyn, was the true shape of the thing Iris had sent him down into the dead world to learn.
