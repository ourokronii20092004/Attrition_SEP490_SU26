# Ch03 · The Leash

The stair went down further than a stair had any business going, and it changed as it went.

At the top it was a made thing, dressed steps cut clean into the foundation of the wall, the work of masons who had known their trade. But the deeper he descended the older the work became, or perhaps only the more abused by whatever had happened to the world — the steps heaved and split, the walls to either side closing in and then falling away into dark he couldn't sound, the air growing colder and thicker and somehow more *kept*, the way the air of a cellar is kept, an air that has not been disturbed in a very long time and resents the disturbing. He kept one hand on the wall. The stone was cold enough to ache through his palm. He counted the steps for a while, because counting was a thing to do that wasn't listening to the dry cracking whisper that had followed him to the stairhead and then, mercifully, stopped — the dead, it seemed, did not take the stairs; they leaned, but they did not climb — and then he lost the count somewhere past two hundred and let it go.

And then the stair ended, and the dark opened out, and he came down into a place that had been underground when the world was alive and was open to the bruised sky now because the world above it had fallen in.

It had been a cistern. He knew it the way he knew things here, without the knowing's source — a vast vaulted cavern built to hold a city's water, the roof a great span of arched stone now broken in three places where the streets above had collapsed down into it, letting columns of the dim violet light fall in slanting shafts onto a floor of cracked stone basins, dry as bone, dry for an age. The light came down in those three shafts and pooled and did not spread, so that the cistern was a place of pale violet islands in a sea of dark, and he stood at the foot of the stair in one of the islands and was, for the first time since the lane, grateful — grateful to be out of the open streets, off the leaning avenues, under a roof even a broken one.

And then he understood that the gratitude was a trap of its own, because under a roof meant the dead were below him as well as around him, folded down into the dry basins where they had gone to shelter in the last hours and been stopped, and that *out of the open* was not a thing this place actually offered anywhere, only the appearance of it, only long enough to lower your guard.

He didn't lower his guard. He found a basin with a high cracked lip, set his back against the stone of it so that nothing could come at him unseen from behind, and stayed standing.

"This will do," Iris said. "Stop here. Before you go any deeper I'm going to teach you to use what I'm lending you, because if I don't, the first time you reach for it under pressure you'll do one of two things — either nothing at all, and stand there fumbling while something slow and patient closes the distance, or far too much, all at once, and put exactly the light in the window I told you not to. And of those two, the second is much worse. So. We do it now, here, where there's nothing close enough to reach you for a few minutes, and you learn the size of it with no one's life riding on the lesson. Sit down. You don't have to. But it helps, the first time, to be still and low and not bracing your whole body — and there's genuinely nothing here that'll touch you before we're done."

He looked around once more — the dark, the slanting violet shafts, the dry basins and their folded sleepers — and then he slid down with his back against the cracked lip and sat, his knees up, and waited.

"Hold out your hand."

He held out his hand. It shook. He looked at it shaking, the fine constant tremor of cold and exhaustion and a fear he'd been carrying so long it had stopped feeling like fear and started feeling like weather, and he did not try to still it or hide it.

"Good," Iris said, and there was something almost like approval in it. "You're not going to pretend to me that your hand is steady. You'd be amazed how many people do — put on the brave face for a voice that has no face to put one on for. It wastes effort and it tells me nothing true. A man who'll let me see his hand shake is a man I can actually work with, because I'll always know where he is." A pause, a choosing-pause. "Now. The mana is already in you. I put a little there while you were waking — primed the pump, you'd say, if you had pumps. So you're not reaching for something far off. You're reaching for something that's already yours to spend, sitting just behind your breastbone, waiting to be asked. Close your hand. And want a small light. Not a fire. Not a torch. A light the size of a coin. Want it *exactly* that big, and not one hair bigger."

---

He closed his hand and wanted a light the size of a coin.

For a moment, nothing — and the nothing was humiliating in a way he had not expected, a hot childish certainty rising in him that he would be the one it didn't work for, the failed case, the warm anomaly that turned out, when tested, to be useless after all. He pushed the certainty down. He tried again, and this time he stopped reaching *outward*, stopped groping for something at a distance, and instead felt *inward*, behind the breastbone where she'd said, and there it was: not in his hand at all but in his chest, a warmth with a grain to it, a thing with a texture, something that had been sitting there quietly the whole time exactly as she'd promised, waiting to be asked.

He took hold of it the way you take hold of a held breath when you've decided where to let it go. And it moved. That was the thing that undid him a little — it *went* where he sent it, down the channel of his arm, smooth, obedient, like water finding the groove you've dug for it. He brought it down into his palm. He opened his hand.

There was a light in it.

The size of a coin. Pale gold, steady, small, throwing a clean warm circle across his palm and up onto his face and out onto the cracked stone of the basin and the dust and the dark — the first real color the place had shown him since he woke, the first warm light, gold against the endless bruise and gray.

He stared at it. After everything — the sky, the wall and its house-sized claw marks, the leaning dead, the Echo and its borrowed despair — it was the small gold coin of light in his own cupped hand that reached him, that put a sudden ache in his throat, because it was *his*. He had made it. Out of wanting. The first thing he had added to this world instead of a thing the world had taken from him or shown him or done to him. A small warm light, the size of a coin, made out of wanting it precisely.

"There," Iris said, and she let him have it — let the moment stand, three full seconds of it, the gold in his hand and the wonder on his face, before she spoke again. "That's the whole of it. That's everything I'm going to teach you, in one coin of light. Wanting, shaped, and sized, and aimed. You'll learn to make it do bigger things — a push, a mend, a holding-shut — but they're all this. A want, made exact. The exactness is the entire skill, the whole of it, start to finish. Anyone can want. Almost no one can want *precisely* — can hold a desire to a fixed size and not let it swell the moment it starts to feel good. And the ones who can't are precisely the ones who put a light in the window." A beat, and the voice shifted, went careful and deliberate. "Now. I'm going to show you the edge. Once. So that you respect it for the rest of this, and so that we never have to go near it again. Make the light bigger. As big as you can. Don't be careful — I want you to feel, in your own body, exactly what careful is *for*."

---

He made the light bigger.

And it was easy — that was the first thing, the thing that frightened him most afterward: that bigger was *easier* than small. The small coin had taken concentration, a constant gentle pinching-down to hold it to its size. Bigger asked for none of that. He simply stopped pinching, and let the want grow, and it *leapt* — eager, almost grateful, as though the warmth in his chest had been straining against the coin-sized leash and lunged the moment it was loosed. The coin became a fist. The fist became a head, a globe, a small sun cupped and burning in his palm, and it threw the whole broken cistern into gold — the slanting violet shafts swallowed up, the dry basins leaping into hard-edged relief, the folded gray dead in them flinging long shadows up the curved walls, shadows where for an age there had been no shadows because there had been no light to make them.

And it kept coming. That was the seduction laid bare: it poured out of him, more and brighter, warm and good and *easy*, the best he had felt since he woke, the pure animal relief of a held thing finally let go, of a clenched fist finally opening, and some part of him wanted to laugh with the simple goodness of it, to let it grow and grow and fill the whole dead world with gold —

And the sky flinched.

That was the only word he ever found for it, and it was never enough. High up, through the largest of the three broken places in the roof, the bruised violet that had hung dead and motionless since the instant he woke *moved*. A slow vast attention swung across the gap — like the beam of something enormous turning over in its sleep, like an eye opening a fraction in a face the size of the horizon — and the flat sweet air of the cistern went suddenly, horribly thin, and a pressure came down on the inside of his skull. Not pain. Worse than pain, because pain at least is a thing happening to a part of you; this happened to *all* of him at once, gentle and total and unspeakably large: the pressure of being *noticed*. Of being, for one instant, a detail that something had not previously bothered to resolve, and was now, slowly, beginning to.

"Put it out." Iris's voice had not risen. But everything easy had gone out of it, every thread of dryness, and what was left was flat and fast and absolutely certain. "Now. Small. Coin. *Now.*"

He crushed the want back down.

It fought him. That was the horror of the lesson, the part that lived in his body afterward and that no telling could have given him: that bigger did not *want* to become small again, that the channel he'd dug ran the wrong way now, downhill toward more, and forcing it back uphill toward less was like trying to make a flood climb back into a cup. For one bad second the light guttered and *flared* instead of shrinking, surging brighter as he wrestled it, and the pressure in his skull jumped, his eyes watering, his teeth aching, the attention overhead sharpening by a degree — and then he got his will all the way around it, clamped down, *forced* it, and it collapsed: globe to head to fist to coin to spark to nothing, and his hand was empty and dark and shaking, both hands now, all of him, his back jammed hard against the cold basin and his breath coming in ragged fog.

Above, through the broken roof, the violet went still again.

The pressure lifted, slowly, like a held breath releasing — the air thickening back to its flat sweetness, the vast attention sliding off him, turning back, incurious, to whatever it was that a thing that large did in the dark when it was not being given a reason to do otherwise. Whatever had half-woken at the gold turned over and went back under.

He sat with his back against the stone and breathed and did not speak for a while. Iris let him. She did not fill the silence, which he was learning was its own kind of statement from her — she spoke when there was something worth saying and not before, and her silences meant *I am letting you have this*, and he was grateful for it, the way he'd been grateful for the cold.

"That," she said at last, when his breathing had come back down, "was small. I want you to understand that, and carry it. That was *nothing*. That was a man making a reading-lamp too bright in a cellar. And you felt how far it reached, and how fast, and what turned its head. Now you know — in your body, not just your ears — and I'll never have to tell you twice. That's the only reason I let you do it. Telling you would have cost us an hour of you not quite believing me, testing the edges, wanting the exact number. Showing you cost thirty seconds and a bad fright. You'll forgive me the fright."

"It looked at me," he said. His voice was unsteady.

"It looked at the *light*," she said. "Keep the difference, because the difference is the thing that keeps you alive. It does not know you exist. It knows that for thirty seconds there was a warmth in a place that is supposed to be cold, and that now there isn't, and that knowledge is already sliding off it — because it does not care, because nothing that large cares about anything as small as you, until you make yourself briefly impossible to ignore. So don't. That's all the rule is. Stay small enough to be ignored. You felt the edge tonight. Don't come back to it. Not unless the only other thing on the far side of the choice is dying — and even then, even *then*, think first. You think well. Thinking is the gift, not the power. Use the gift, and borrow the power as little as you can stand to."

---

When his hands had stopped shaking he asked the question that had been sitting under all the others since the square.

"How long."

"Until the fragment reaches my world." Not a flicker of pretending to misunderstand; she never pretended to misunderstand, he was learning, never bought herself a moment that way. "And I've told you I won't give you a number, and I'll tell you again why, because you're the kind of man who'll keep asking a fair question until he gets a real answer instead of a kind one. I can't give you a number I'd stand behind. The drift isn't even — it speeds when it nears certain things and slows in the open dark, and it's far out still, which is the only reason any of this is possible. If I gave you a number, one of two things would happen. Either it would sound long, and you'd dawdle, and the drift would speed when you weren't looking and take the choice out of both our hands. Or it would sound short, and you'd despair, and a man in a hurry in here makes exactly the mistakes this place is built to harvest. So instead of a number I'll give you the truth, and the truth is: long enough to do this properly if you don't waste it, and not long enough to do it twice. That's the clock. I'm sorry it isn't kinder. I've found that lying about clocks is the single cruelest thing you can do to a person who's standing on one."

He turned it over, and as he turned it he examined himself for the despair she'd warned against, and found — to his own faint surprise — that it wasn't there. And then he understood why it wasn't there, and the understanding came out of him slowly, as a kind of cold small comfort that was also, he would realize later, the second thing he'd built himself out of, after the rule on the square.

"It doesn't change anything," he said. "The clock. Whether it's long or short." He frowned at his empty hand, where the light had been. "Either way the only road out is down. You said so — there's no version where I wait it out up here, because waiting up here just makes me furniture. And there's no out going back up, either; the dead lean and the sky flinches and there was never any door for me up there. So the clock doesn't actually give me a decision to make. There's only one road. Long or short just tells me how fast to walk it. I don't have to be brave about it. I just have to notice there's only the one road, and stop wasting strength wishing there were two."

The cistern was quiet. Above, the dead light hung still in the three broken places in the roof. Somewhere below, faint, a sound had begun that he hadn't noticed under his own ragged breathing.

"Most people," Iris said, and the dryness was back in her voice, but under it was the other thing, the still and attentive thing he'd heard when the first figure turned, "most people have to be frightened into walking down into a place like this, or inspired into it, talked into courage they don't have. You did it with *arithmetic*. You looked at all the exits, found there was exactly one, and decided to be mildly annoyed about it rather than afraid of it." A pause. "I'm going to tell you something true, because you've earned a true thing and because it's the kind that's useful to know about yourself. I have been doing this work a very long time. I am not easily surprised — being surprised is, in a sense, a failure of my attention, and I attend very closely. And I have now been surprised by you four times. The waking. The not-arguing. The not-wasting. And now this — walking toward the worst thing in the world because the sums came out that way and there was nothing to be gained by feeling otherwise. I'm keeping a count. I want you to know that I'm keeping a count, because I think it matters, in the end, that someone was paying enough attention to keep one."

He got to his feet. His legs held — steadier than when he'd sat.

And he noticed, getting up, that her count had done something to him — that the four times she'd been surprised sat warm in his chest in a way that had nothing to do with survival, a small private glow he didn't entirely want to look at. Because here was the thing about a man with no name and no past and no one: tell him he is *good at something*, that he is the rare one, the one who does what others can't, and you have handed him the first solid thing he's ever been allowed to be. He took the warmth of it gladly. He told himself it was nothing — pride was a luxury and he had a dead world to cross — but he kept the count too, after that. Privately. *Four times.* He started, without admitting it, to want a fifth. It is a small flaw, in a man, to like being good at the thing he is good at. It is the kind of small flaw that gets people killed at the bottom of dead worlds, and he could not see that yet, because the thing about that particular flaw is that it feels, from the inside, exactly like competence.

And the sound he'd half-heard resolved as he stood: a rhythmic, distant clang, coming up out of a tunnel at the far end of the cistern where the dry basins gave way to a passage sloping further down. Metal on stone. Metal on metal. The flat repeated cadence of a thing being struck on a count — a drill being called, an order being kept, a discipline being held against the silence.

"That's the Wall," Iris said. "That's the first of the five. His name is Karr, and he was the Shield of this world when it was alive, and he is still — right now, down that tunnel — fighting a war that ended before your language had a word for *war*. He is going to take one look at a living man who can hold a spear and be gladder than you can imagine, and that gladness is going to be the most dangerous thing you've met yet, more dangerous than the sky, because the sky only wants to unmake you and Karr is going to want to *keep* you, for a cause, with his whole grieving heart." A beat. "Remember what I told you in the lane. Don't let him put a spear in your hands before you've understood him. And don't freeze — not in front of that one. He spent a lifetime reading men for the flinch, and an age more drilling the flinch out of the dead, and he can still smell it. It's most of what he has left."

Ren faced the dark tunnel and the far patient clang of a dead man keeping time over a war that was already lost and won and over.

"Spend small," he said, counting them off like a man checking the rungs of a ladder he's about to climb down in the dark. "Don't lie to myself. Don't freeze." Three, now. He was building something. He could feel it taking shape, rung by rung, a self assembled out of rules in a place that wanted him to have none. "All right."

He walked down toward the sound of the war.
