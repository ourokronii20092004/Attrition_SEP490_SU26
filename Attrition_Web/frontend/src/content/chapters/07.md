# Ch07 · The Painless Thing

The corruption came for him in the dark of the ward, and it came wearing Talwyn's voice, and that was how he learned, all the way down, that Iris had been exactly right.

He had not meant to sleep. He had lain on the clean cot with his hands folded on his chest and his cold eye open, meaning to keep it open through whatever the place called night. But the ward was warm, and the lamps were low and amber, and his body had been running on fear and borrowed power for longer than he could count, and somewhere in the long soft hours it simply took what his will would not give it, and he went under.

He surfaced slow, and warm, and wrong.

The wrongness was that he felt *good*. He came up out of sleep into a spreading painless ease, the same warm water as before, and his first muddied thought was gratitude — Talwyn must have come to him in the dark after all, taken his hand while he slept, eased him the way she'd wanted to. He turned his head to find her.

She hadn't. The healer was down at the far end of the ward, bent over another bed, tending. No one was near him. No one had touched him.

The ease was coming from inside.

"Ren." Iris's voice, low and very steady, the steadiness of a person speaking carefully to someone standing somewhere high. "Wake all the way up. Now. Gently — don't thrash. The thing you're feeling isn't her. It's the rot. You slept, and your guard came down with you, and it took the opening you left. It's doing the kind thing. It's taking the pain. And I need you to feel how good it is, right now, and understand in the same breath that *that* is the danger — not teeth, not claws, not a monster. The danger is that it feels exactly, precisely, like being healed by someone who loves you."

---

He sat up, slow, and made himself feel for the place the ease was coming from, and found it, and it horrified him fully awake.

His left hand. The fingers of his left hand had gone — not numb; numb was too small and too honest a word for it — they had gone *quiet*. They looked the same. Still warm to the eye, still his, still attached. But the sensation in them had smoothed away to nothing, the precise smoothness of the living woman's face across the row, and where the feeling should have been there was instead a small, perfect, seductive painlessness that wanted — he could feel it wanting, the way he'd felt the light want to grow — to spread. It had taken the fingers while he slept. It would take the hand, if he let it, and then the wrist, and the forearm, and each piece it took would stop hurting, would stop being cold, would stop being afraid, would stop being able to fail or tire or break — and would stop being *his*, in any way that mattered, would become a quiet deathless instrument bolted onto the side of a living man.

And the worst of it — the part Iris had warned him about and he had not, until this moment, truly believed — was that he could see the *use* of it. A hand that felt no pain. A hand that could not be hurt, could not cramp, could not shake with cold or fear. Down here, against the slow leaning dead, against whatever waited further down — that was an advantage. A real one. He could win small fights with a hand like that, fights his living hand might lose. He could trade the feeling in four fingers for an edge, and the trade would be so *easy*, easier than anything, because the fingers didn't even hurt to give — giving them was the opposite of hurting, giving them was *relief* — and it would only be the fingers, only a little, only the part he could most easily spare —

"That's the voice," Iris said. "*Only a little. Only the part you can spare.* That, exactly, word for word. It never asks for the whole of you. That's the genius of the thing, that's why it's beaten nearly everyone — it doesn't demand, it *offers*, and it offers something that genuinely costs you nothing you can feel, because the part it takes is the part it's already numbed. The fingers don't hurt to give. Giving them is a relief. And then a little while later it asks for the hand, and by then you're a man who has already learned, in his own body, that giving feels like relief — and you've four fewer fingers' worth of yourself left to do the refusing with." A pause. "You're at the first ask, Ren. The cheapest one there will ever be. The fingers. What do you do?"

Ren looked at his quiet left hand in the amber dark.

And then he did a thing that frightened him more than any of the dead had — more than the turned woman, more than Karr crossing the yard. He took hold of the painlessness, the way he'd taken hold of the want for the coin of light, and he forced feeling *back* into it. He drove sensation down into the quiet fingers by an act of pure refusing will — and it came back wrong, came back as pins, as fire, as the deep grinding ache of a hand that had been let go and was now being dragged back into holding on — and it was *agony*, small and stupid and total, four fingers screaming as the feeling flooded back into them, and he sat there in the dark of the ward and *welcomed* it, gritted his teeth and welcomed the pain, because the pain was his and the ease had not been.

"Don't take the painless thing," he said through his teeth. "Not the fingers. Not even the fingers. Not even a little."

"*Not even a little,*" Iris repeated, and there was something in how she said it he had not heard from her before — not the surprise she'd promised to stop voicing, something quieter and more careful underneath it, something almost like the thing a person feels watching another person do the brave thing they themselves had only ever been able to describe. "Most people give the fingers, Ren. Nearly every one. It's such a small ask, and they're so tired, and it costs nothing they can feel. You are the first in a very long time to claw it back once it had already started. I want you to mark that you did that. You're going to need to remember, very soon, that you chose the pain over the ease *on purpose*, with your eyes open — because in a few minutes Talwyn is going to offer you the exact same trade, with her whole loving heart, and it is a great deal harder to refuse a gift than to refuse a thief."

---

Talwyn found him with his teeth gritted and his ruined hand on fire, and she crossed the ward at once, alarmed, all her love mobilizing toward the hurt.

"Child — *child* — what are you doing to yourself, you're hurting, I can feel you hurting from clear across the room, what have you—" Her hands came for his burning hand, to take the pain out of it, and Ren pulled it back against his chest, out of her reach, and the pain of pulling it back was nothing beside the look that put on her face — bewildered, wounded, a healer refused.

"Don't," he said. "I need it. The pain. I need to keep it."

"That is the *sickness* talking—"

"No." He made himself meet her warm wet eyes. "It's the sickness offering to take it, and me saying no. Talwyn — listen to me. I'm not like the others you've kept. I'm warm. Really warm, all the way through, not a cold body you found and tended back from the edge. I'm alive. And I think you've already felt that, the way you feel a fever — and I think some part of you has already started, somewhere underneath, thinking about how you might keep me. The way you kept her." He nodded toward the smooth-faced woman across the row. "Forever. Safe. Never hurting. Don't answer yet — I'm not asking you to answer. I'm just telling you what you're about to offer me, out loud, while we both still can, so that when you offer it, we both know exactly what it is."

"I would take such good care of you," Talwyn whispered, and it was the most loving and the most frightening sentence anyone had ever said to him, the two things perfectly fused. "You'd never be cold again. Never afraid. I'd mind you, always, I'd never let the rot take another inch of you, you would just — rest. After everything you've carried — wouldn't that be — after all of it, wouldn't that be a *mercy*—"

"It would be the same as her." Ren kept his eyes on the smooth living face across the row, the open untenanted eyes, the breath going in and out of a person who was no longer home to receive it. "Wouldn't it. That's what taking such good care of me would come to, in the end. I'd lie in that bed with my face gone smooth and I'd never hurt and I'd never be cold and I'd never be afraid and I'd never be *anyone* again. You wouldn't be saving me, Talwyn. You'd be doing to me the exact thing you did to her, and calling it love — the way you've called it love every single time, all the way back to the first."

"*Stop,*" Talwyn said, and her voice broke clean across the middle. "Stop saying that. I keep them. I keep them *safe.* That's not — I'm not the thing out there, I'm not the rot, I'm its opposite, I'm the one who stands against it, I—"

"You're not its opposite," Ren said, as gently as he had ever said anything in his short remembered life. "That's the part I'm sorry about. That's the part that's going to be hard. You're the *same* as it. The rot wants to take away pain, and fear, and dying — and so do you. And you're both completely right that those things hurt, that they're terrible, that a person in their grip is suffering. And you're both completely wrong that taking them away is a mercy — because pain and fear and dying are what *living costs*, they're the price of the thing itself, and a person who's been relieved of all three isn't safe, Talwyn. They're not saved. They're just *finished*, early, gently, by someone who loved them too much to let them pay the price of staying real. You haven't been keeping them. You've been ending them. Slower than the rot does it, and kinder, and calling the slowness *winning.*"

The healer stood in the amber light with her loving hands open and empty at her sides, and the lid she had kept clamped over her grief since a long-ago dawn began, finally, to fail.

---

"Then I lost," Talwyn said. The word came up out of her like something pulled out by the root, dirt and all. "If you're right. If keeping them was never saving them. Then I didn't win all this time — I *lost*. Every one. All of them. I lost them the way I lost her, every single time, and I just — I wouldn't *look* at it. I told myself I was winning so I'd never have to—"

"You didn't lose her because you failed," Ren said. "That's the thing I don't think anyone has ever been allowed to say to you, because you never let them get close enough to say it. You held your daughter all that night and you did everything a healer could do and everything a mother could do, and she died anyway — and that was not you failing. That was the worst thing that can happen to a person, happening to you. There's no skill that saves everyone. There's no being good enough that death stops coming. She didn't die because you weren't enough, Talwyn. She died because sometimes children die, and it is the cruelest fact there is, and it was *not your failure to fix.* It was just the worst thing. And you were allowed to have just — lost her. Not lost-as-in-failed. Lost-as-in-grieved."

Talwyn made a sound he hoped never to hear again, a sound an age in the making.

"You're allowed to put her down," he said, over it, into it, the way you keep talking steadily to someone going under cold water. "Not down-as-in-forgotten. Down-as-in-grieved. You're allowed to feel what that dawn felt like, instead of spending forever making certain no one else ever feels it. The never-feeling-it is the thing that's killing you — and it's the thing that's killing everyone you keep, because you're keeping them so you never have to feel it, and that's not for them, Talwyn, it was never for them, it was always to keep yourself from that one dawn. You can stop. You held the line longer than anyone could have asked — longer than Karr held his, and his was an age. But it was never a line that could be held. And you're allowed to stand down off it, the same as he was. You're allowed to grieve her. You're allowed to let them all go. Including, finally, yourself."

"I don't know how," Talwyn whispered. "I've held it so long I don't — I don't know how to let go of even one. I've forgotten the shape of letting go. It was the first thing I unlearned."

"Yes you do," Ren said. "You've always known how. You just decided, at one dawn, with your whole soul, that you never would. So un-decide it. That's the whole of it. Not all at once. Just — once. Just let one go, and see that you survive it. Start with one."

And Talwyn, the Hearth of a dead world, who had not let a single soul go since the morning her daughter died because she had refused, with everything in her, to let losing be a thing that could happen — turned to the living woman across the row. The one she had kept smooth and safe and empty for an age. And she went to her. And she sat on the edge of that bed the way she must once have sat on the edge of a much smaller one at a long-ago dawn, and she took the woman's slack warm hand in both of hers — and this time she did not pour her own life in to hold it smooth.

This time she let go.

What Ren had braced himself for — that releasing the kept survivors meant killing them, that he had just talked a grieving woman into euthanizing the people she'd saved — turned out to be both truer and far gentler than he'd feared. The woman across the row did not die. She came *back.* As Talwyn stopped holding her smooth, stopped pouring in the ease, the emptiness behind the open eyes flickered — and filled — and became a person: confused, blinking, surfacing up out of an age of enforced painlessness into the cold sharp astonishing fact of being alive again. Of being able to feel the sheets. To feel cold. To feel afraid. To be able, someday, to die — and therefore, again, finally, to live. She drew a breath that was her own, the first in an age that belonged to the person drawing it. And she began, quietly, to weep — and the weeping was the most alive thing Ren had seen since he woke, because it was a person *feeling* something at last, something real, something that hadn't been smoothed away the instant it formed.

"That's the mercy you were afraid of," Iris said softly. "You thought letting go meant ending them. For the ones still alive under all that keeping, letting go meant *giving them back.* Not all of them — some are too far gone, kept too long, there's no one left inside to give back to. But some aren't. Talwyn will know which is which; she always knew, underneath, she just couldn't bear to act on the knowing. Remember this, Ren. Remember it specifically, all the way down to the bottom, because it is going to matter more than you can yet see what releasing a kept thing actually does. It is not always a killing. Sometimes — when the thing was only being held, not yet destroyed — it's the opposite. Sometimes letting go is how you give a person back to themselves."

Talwyn was weeping too now, finally, an age of dammed grief breaking and coming out of her all at once — and she did not look, weeping, like a woman dying of it. She looked like someone who had set down a weight that had bent her double for so long she had forgotten she was bent, and was discovering, astonished, that her spine still knew how to straighten. When she had quieted, she looked up at Ren, and the brightness in her face was gone for good, and what had replaced it was something worn and real and, for the first time, at peace.

"There are others I've kept," she said. "Down the ward. Some are too far — there's no one left in them to give back, and for those, the kindest thing is to finally let them finish, the way you let Karr finish. But some aren't too far. Some can still come back. I'll know which. I always knew which." Her voice steadied. "Will you let me do the last of my work properly, this time? Give back the ones that can come back. Let go of the ones that can't. It's the work I should have been doing all along — it *is* healing, it was always healing, I only had the direction wrong. I thought healing meant never letting go. It was always knowing *when.*"

"Yes," Ren said. "That's exactly what it is. That's the whole of what it is. That's healing."

---

He left her to it — moving down the rows of her ward, bed by bed, doing at last the hardest part of a healer's work, the part she had refused at a dawn an age ago: knowing when to hold on, and knowing when the holding had become the harm, and having the courage to tell the difference and act on it.

The amber light did not dim as he went. It only — eased, the way the ward itself eased, a place that had been straining every moment to keep something now simply warm, simply at rest, a hearth instead of a held breath.

His left hand still hurt. He was fiercely glad of it. He flexed the fingers — they were his again, every aching one of them, every pin and needle his own — and he found the stair at the far end of the ward, going down, and from below this time there was no warmth and no humming. From below rose a light that was not amber but white-gold, and trembling, and many voices lifted together — joyful, certain, unafraid — *singing.*

"The third," Iris said. "His name is Bran. He was the Flame." A pause, and her voice did the careful, bracing thing again. "Karr's pain was on the outside, where you could see it. Talwyn's kindness was the trap. This one is worse than either, and I want you to know why before you go down, because this is the one that's going to tempt *you* to do the cruel thing instead of the kind one — the reverse of every stratum so far. Down there, everyone is happy. Down there, everyone is *saved.* They sing because they know — they *know*, with their whole hearts — that the rescue is coming. Any moment now. It's been any-moment for an age, and not one of them has been afraid for a single hour of it, because Bran gave them the most beautiful lie that has ever been told, and they believe it down to the soles of their feet." A long pause. "And you're going to have to decide whether to take it from them. That's the trap this time. Not a comfort offered to you. A comfort you'll have to choose whether to *destroy* — in people who are, every one of them, grateful for it."

Ren stood at the top of the stair, and listened to the dead sing, and for the first time since he woke under the bruised sky he was not at all sure his rules had an answer ready for what waited below.

He went down toward the singing anyway. That was a rule too, maybe — the first one, underneath all the others, the one he'd made before he'd known he was making any. *You go down.*
