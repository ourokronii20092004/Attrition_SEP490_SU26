# Ch02 · The First Dead

The wall did not get closer for a long time, and then it got closer all at once.

That was the first thing the dead world taught him about distance, and it taught it slowly, in the way of a lesson you don't know you're being given until it's already done with you. He set out across the broken square toward the near wall — the one where the ground fell away, where the voice had said there was a stair — and he walked, and the wall stayed where it was. He walked further. It stayed. He could feel his legs doing the work, feel the cold paving give way to cold bare ground and then to the rubble of streets, because they were streets, he understood now; he had woken not in a field but in a city, in the open square at the heart of one, and the square gave onto avenues that ran out toward the distant dark. He walked along one of them for what his body insisted was a long time, and the great wall at the end of it did not grow.

And then he came around the shell of a building — a vast collapsed hall, its roof fallen in, its doorway a black mouth he gave a wide berth — and the wall was simply *there*. Filling the sky from edge to edge. So high and so near that he stopped dead and threw a hand up, the same flinch the frozen figure in the square wore, an animal certainty that the thing was going to come down on him. It did not come down. Nothing here came down. Everything here had already done its falling, an age ago, and been forbidden to finish.

He stood in the shadow of it — except there was no shadow, he realized, looking for the cool dark strip a wall that size should have thrown; the dim violet light came from everywhere and nowhere at once and cast nothing, so that he stood at the foot of a thing taller than any made structure he could imagine and it laid no shade on him at all, and the wrongness of that, the missing shadow, unsettled him as much as anything had. He looked up the face of the wall, keeping his eyes carefully on the stone and off the sky above its rim.

And he saw the marks.

---

Gouges. The whole lower reach of the wall, as far up as he could see before the stone met the bruise of the sky, was raked with them — deep parallel furrows cut into stone that should have turned any blade, any ram, any engine of siege that the people who built it could have conceived. The stone was the same dark basalt-looking rock as everything else, hard, meant to last, meant to *hold* — and something had drawn its claws down the face of it the way a man draws his nails down wet clay.

Some of the gouges were the length of his arm. He could have lain down inside the longer ones. And some — high up, far past the reach of any ladder, any scaffold, any siege-tower — some were the size of houses, four clean parallel trenches dragged down through the rock in a single motion, as though a hand the width of a street had laid its fingers flat against the wall and simply *pulled*, peeling stone away in ribbons, and then moved on, incurious, to do the same somewhere else.

He stood and looked at them and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do — the span of the four trenches, the gap between them, the size of the hand that gap implied, the size of the thing that hand belonged to — and his mind shied off the answer the way his eyes shied off the sky.

He had not said anything. But the voice answered the thing he had not said.

"Yes," Iris said. "Those. You're right to look at those and go quiet. Going quiet is the correct response to those. I'd be worried about you if you'd had any other."

"What made them," he said.

"Something that isn't in here with you." A pause, and then, in the tone of a person choosing precision on purpose: "I'm going to be careful how I answer that, because I've learned that if I leave a gap, a mind fills it with the worst thing it can imagine — and the worst thing you can imagine, right now, is smaller than the truth, so leaving the gap would actually mislead you. So I'll just say it. There are things out in the dark around this fragment that are large enough to do that to a city wall and not notice they had done it. Not as an act of war. As a man's boot does that to an anthill he didn't see. They are not coming in — I've told you why, and the why hasn't changed. The marks are old. The thing that made them is elsewhere now, being patient in the way that kind of thing is patient, which is the patience of something that has never once been in a hurry because it has never once been at risk of not getting what it waits for." A beat. "Walk on. And don't make a habit of standing still in the open, looking up. I'll tell you why in a moment, and you're not going to like it."

He walked on, along the base of the clawed wall, and he tried not to picture the hand, and failed, and pictured it, and then put it down with an effort and kept his eyes on the way ahead.

---

The streets here, close under the wall, were fuller than the square had been.

In the open square the stopped figures had been scattered, a few dozen across a wide space, lonely in their stillness. Here, in the close lanes that fed toward the wall, they crowded — and they were all, he saw, facing the same way, all caught heading in a single direction, toward a gate that stood open in the wall ahead of him and let onto nothing but more gray beyond. A press of people, frozen in flight. He walked up among them, into the middle of them, because the lane gave him no other path, and he made himself look, because not-looking was its own kind of cowardice and he had a rule now about the shapes fear took.

They were ordinary. That was the thing that got into him. Not soldiers, not monsters, not anything grand — ordinary people, in the ordinary clothes of whatever ordinary day this had been before it became the last one. A woman with a child hoisted onto her hip, her free hand reaching back for someone behind her, her mouth open on a word, a name probably, frozen half-shouted. A man half-turned to look over his shoulder at the thing that was coming, his face caught in the instant before the face knows to be afraid, when it is still only puzzled. An old one, fallen to the stones, and three others stopped in the act of bending to lift them — strangers, maybe, or family, you couldn't tell, people who in the last seconds of a dying city had stopped their own running to help someone who'd gone down. All of them frost-furred. All of them gray. All of them held, forever, in the last ordinary second before the unspeakable thing arrived.

"They were trying to get out," he said. His voice had gone low.

"They were."

"Did any of them—"

"No," Iris said. "Don't ask me that one all the way to the end. I'll answer it if you make me, but the answer is the same for every figure in this lane and it will not help you carry what you're already carrying. Keep walking. The gate's ahead. Go through it."

He went toward the gate, through the crowd of the frozen, and he noticed the woman with the child because she was directly in his path and he had to step around her — close, the lane was narrow, close enough to see the fine frost furred on the child's eyelashes — and he gave her the wide berth he'd given all of them, some courtesy he could not have explained toward a person who could no longer be discomfited by a stranger passing too near, and he went on six or seven steps past her.

And then a wrongness pulled at the back of his neck. The particular wrongness of being looked at. He turned.

The woman had turned too.

Not far — a few degrees, no more, the frost on her cracking in a fine pale dust where the motion had broken it, a little fall of it drifting down off her shoulder. But where her open mouth and her reaching hand and her hoisted child had all been facing the gate, the way out, the direction of the life she had been trying so hard to reach — now they faced *him*. The child's head had come around on her hip. Two faces, mother and infant, gray and blank and frost-rimed, angled toward the one warm thing in the lane.

He went very still. And then remembered, an instant too late, that he had made a rule about exactly this, and made himself unstill — took a step back instead, deliberately, a chosen motion, not a flinch.

Behind the woman, three more of them had turned.

---

"There it is," Iris said, soft. "Now you understand why I don't want you standing still in the open. And here's the part you won't like, so I'll say it quickly and plainly. You're warm. You are the only warm thing that has happened in this place in longer than your language has a word for. And the dead in here — they aren't hunting you. Don't picture hunting; there's not enough left in them to hunt. But they *lean*. Heat is the last thing a body remembers wanting, the very last, after the name and the face and the why are all gone — and they remember it the way a plant remembers the sun. No thought in it. No malice. Just a turning-toward. You stand still long enough, they all turn toward you. You stand still longer, they come. Not fast — there's nothing fast left in them. But there are a very great many of them, and they don't tire, and they don't stop, and a slow thing that never tires and never stops is, in the end, how most of the people who came before you actually died. Not torn apart. Just — caught. By the slow patient lean of a whole dead city that wanted to be near the warm."

He was already moving — out from among the turned faces, out under the open gate, into a wider street beyond it — and walking fast, and behind him now he could hear it, the thing he hadn't heard before because he hadn't known to listen for it: a small dry continuous sound, the sound of frost cracking, of gray limbs unsticking from postures held for an age, a faint collective shifting as, behind him, the crowd of the stopped began — without hurry, without sound but the cracking of their own frost — to follow the warm thing that had passed through them.

And under the dry collective shuffle of it, once, he heard something that did not fit.

A single tread. Further back than the rest, beyond the leaning crowd, in the deeper dark of the lanes he'd already come through — a footfall, and then another, even, unhurried, *deliberate*, with a weight and a rhythm that had nothing of the shuffling lean about it. The dead did not walk like that. The dead did not walk at all; they leaned, and shifted, and were drawn. This was a thing *crossing a room toward something it had decided on.* He stopped, despite the rule, and listened — and it stopped too, the instant he did, the way only a thing that knows it's being listened for stops. The dry shuffle of the crowd went on. The single tread did not resume. He stood in the cold with his heart going hard and the back of his neck telling him, in the old animal language, that somewhere behind him in the dark something that was not leaning had turned to face him on purpose — and then he made himself move again, because standing was what fear wanted, and the sound did not come again, and he told himself he had imagined it.

He had not imagined it. But it would be a long way down before he learned that, and longer still before he learned whose tread it was.

He did not look back. Looking back was a thing fear wanted and he was learning what fear wanted.

"They came before me," he said instead, to keep his mind on a track that wasn't the dry cracking sound. "You said most of the people who came before me. So there were people. Before me. You sent people into this."

A silence. It had a different texture than her other silences — those were choosing-silences, weighing how to say a thing. This one was a deciding-silence, weighing whether to.

"Yes," Iris said. "I did. And we'll talk about it — I'm not going to pretend I didn't say it, and I'm not going to pretend you didn't hear it. But not now, in a lane, with the dead at your heels. There's an order to hard things and that one comes later. Right now you have a nearer problem, and it's the gray smudge in the air about forty feet ahead of you that you've been walking straight toward without seeing. Stop. Look at it. That's the other kind of thing in here, and it's worth knowing on sight, because you'll meet a great many of them and the first one shouldn't be a surprise."

---

He had taken it for smoke.

It hung in the middle of the wider street at about the height of a standing man — a stain on the air, a smudge of the gray-violet of the sky condensed down into a vague and faintly shifting blot, denser at its heart and feathering at its edges into nothing. He had been walking toward it without registering it, the way the eye slides off a thing it has no category for. Now he stopped and made himself look, and the longer he looked the less it resembled smoke. Smoke moved, drifted, dispersed. This did none of those, because nothing here moved the air — and yet it was not quite still either; it *roiled*, slowly, in place, turning over on itself, and the more he looked the more it seemed to him not like a thing in the air but like a *place where something had been removed* — the after-image a thing leaves when it has been taken out of the world too thoroughly to leave even a body. A hole shaped like a grief.

"What is it," he said.

"An Echo," Iris said. "When something dies badly enough in here — when the dying is the only thing left of it, when there's no self to loop, no gesture to freeze, just the misery, distilled — that's what stays. The grief without the person. It has no body. It has no wants, no mind, nothing you could bargain with or be hurt by in any way you'd call being hurt. It can't touch you. But it remembers being a feeling, and it leans toward the real thing the way the dead lean toward your heat — and if you walk through it, you'll feel, for a moment, what it was the last of. Most people feel it for a second and come out the other side and it's gone. Some people feel it, and stand there in the middle of it feeling it, and don't come out, and stand there until the slow things catch up." A beat. "Go around it. It isn't a test and there's nothing to learn in there but the exact shape of one stranger's worst moment, and you'll have enough of those of your own before we're through. Just go around."

He went around. The street was wide enough, but not wide enough to go far around, and as he passed it — six feet away, then four — the trailing edge of the thing reached the edge of him, the feathering gray brushing against his arm, and for the space of one single step he felt it.

It was not an image. There was no picture, no voice, no scene. It was a feeling, arriving whole and entire and uninvited, occupying him completely for the length of that one step: the precise interior texture of a particular despair, a specific person's specific last understanding. *The gate is too far. I have done the sum and the gate is too far and I am not going to reach it. The thing behind me is going to reach me first and there is nothing left to do about it and the last thing that is going to happen to me is*—

And then he was past it, out the far side, and the feeling let go of him all at once, and it was his own fear again, only his own, the ordinary baseline dread he'd been carrying since he woke — and his own fear, after that one borrowed second, felt almost like relief. He stood breathing for a moment. He did not look back at the Echo. He understood, now, why some people stopped inside them. There was a terrible completion in it. It was someone else's, and it was finished, and his own fear was neither.

---

He came to the next corner and put a hand against cold stone and stood, just for a moment, to let his heart settle. Behind him the followed crowd came on — slow, patient, leaning, the dry crack of their frost a faint continuous whisper in the still air. Ahead, the wider street ran on toward the wall, and at the wall's base, he could see now, the dark mouth of a stair, cut down into the foundation, going below.

"You're learning the place fast," Iris said. "Faster than I'd have put money on, and I don't lose bets often. Most people spend the whole of the first day insisting it can't be what it plainly is — arguing with the dead, demanding the rules be different, wasting hours the place is happy to take from them. You skipped all of that. You looked at it and you took it as said."

"It doesn't matter what I insist." His voice came out flatter than he'd meant it. He was tired, he realized, in a way that had nothing to do with the walking. "It's still what it is. Insisting just wastes the time you told me I don't have twice."

"No," she agreed, and there it was again, the small sharpening, the interest she didn't trouble to hide. "It's still what it is. Hold onto that one. *It's still what it is.* I think it's going to turn out to be the single most useful thing about you — more useful than the rules, more useful than anything I can lend you. A man who can look at a true thing he hates and not waste a breath pretending it's otherwise can do almost anything that's actually possible to do. The trouble most people have isn't courage. It's that they spend all their strength arguing with the situation instead of solving it." A pause. "The stair goes down to the next layer. There's someone down there, and he is going to be very, very glad to see a new body that can hold a spear. Don't let him put one in your hands before you've understood him. He isn't a monster. I'll tell you that now and I'll tell you again, because you're going to want him to be one, and he isn't, and none of them are, and that — that is the entire problem with this place. If any of them were monsters, this would be easy, and I could have sent anyone, and the five before you would have lived."

He looked down the stair, into a dark the dim violet didn't reach.

"Walk," she said. And then: "And Ren—"

He went still. It was the first time she had used the name. And he was almost certain — he turned it over even as the dark of the stair waited in front of him — he was almost certain that he had never told it to her. That he had not, in fact, known it himself until she said it; that it had arrived in him only now, in her voice, as though she had handed it to him along with everything else.

He filed it. With the marks on the wall, and the things outside, and the people who came before, and the *we* she kept not-quite-saying — he filed the fact that the voice had known his name before he did, in the growing drawer of things about her that did not add up.

"Don't freeze," she said. "You already know it. I'm only saying it because it gets harder from here, not easier, and the first stratum is the one where a man who's done well so far gets careless because he's done well so far. Don't. Go down."

He went down.
