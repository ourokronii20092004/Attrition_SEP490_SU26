# Ch01 · Arrival

The first thing was the sky, and the sky was wrong.

He came up into awareness the way a drowned man comes up into air — not gently, not by degrees, but all at once, breaching some surface he hadn't known he was under, and the first thing waiting for him on the other side of it was the color overhead, and the color was wrong in a way that reached down past his eyes and laid a cold hand on something older than thought.

It was the color of a bruise three days old. That deep blackish violet that is not quite purple and not quite anything, the shade a thing turns when the blood beneath it has stopped moving and begun, quietly, to go bad. He had seen that color before, somewhere, on something — he could not have said where, the memory was a shape with no contents — but his body knew it, and his body did not like it, and that was how he learned the first fact about himself in this place: that he had a body, and that it had opinions, and that it had begun, already, to be afraid.

It hung low, the sky. That was the wrongness he registered before he had words for any of the rest of it — not the color first but the *nearness*, the way it pressed down close the way a ceiling is close, a lid laid over the world at a height a thrown stone could reach. He had the sudden conviction that if he stood and reached up he could touch it, and the conviction was so strong and so awful that he kept his arm where it was, flat against cold ground, because some animal part of him understood that he must not, that touching it would be the worst mistake a person could make, though he could not have said why.

There were no clouds in it. He looked for them — the eye wants clouds, wants the sky to be doing something, going somewhere — and there were none, and there were no stars either, and there was no sun, no moon, no source for the dim even light that lay over everything. There was nothing in it at all. It did not move. And the longer he looked the more he felt that the nothing was not empty but *attentive*, that the flat violet vault was looking back at him without eyes and without interest, the way deep water looks at the small warm things that fall into it — not with malice, malice would have been a kind of relationship, but with the vast patient indifference of something that will have you eventually and is in no hurry, because where else, in the end, are you going to go.

He looked away from it.

He did this without deciding to. Some part of him below the part that was still assembling itself simply turned his eyes down and away, the way you snatch a hand back from a flame, and the relief of not-looking was so immediate and so total that he understood he had been right to be afraid — that to keep looking at that sky was to come apart, slowly, from the top of the mind down, and that the animal in him had known it and saved him before the rest of him had finished waking.

And that was the second fact, and it was a stranger one than the first: that he had a part of him that could come apart. Which meant there was something there to do the coming. Which meant he *was*. He existed. He lay on cold ground beneath a bruised and lidded sky, and he was a person, a single specific person, and he could not — for a long swimming moment in which he searched himself the way you pat down your own pockets in a panic — he could not produce any other thing about that person that he knew for certain. Not a name. Not a face. Not a where, or a how, or a why. Only the bare fact of the searching, and the one who searched.

It should have frightened him more than it did. He noted that, distantly. A man who wakes with no name in no place under no sky should be undone by it, and he was not undone, and the not-being-undone was itself a small cold clue about who he might turn out to be — that somewhere under the blankness was a thing that, handed an impossible situation, did not scream but began, quietly, to take inventory.

But under the inventory-taking, lower down, where he could not quite get a look at it, there was something else, and it was not cold at all. It was a kind of hunger, and it frightened him more than the sky had — because he could feel its shape without being able to see its face, and the shape of it was *want.* Not for food, not for warmth, though he wanted those too. It wanted to be *someone.* To have been somebody, before this — to have a name that another mouth had said, a face some other face had been glad to see, a single thread running back from this cold ground into a life where he had mattered to one other living thing. He had nothing. He was a man with no past and no name and no one, waking alone, and the hunger that woke with him was the simple bottomless wish to not be no-one. He did not like knowing that about himself. It seemed a small and graceless thing to want, with a whole dead world to be afraid of. But it was the truest thing he had found in the searching, and he had already, without a name for the rule yet, decided not to lie to himself, so he let it stand: he wanted to matter to someone. It was the first thing he was, under the method he would build on top of it.

---

Then the cold came, and the cold was the proof.

It had been there the whole time, he realized; he had simply been too busy with the sky to feel it. But now it announced itself, came up out of the ground and into his back, into the knobs of his spine and the flat blades of his shoulders and the backs of his legs, a real cold with a real ache riding on it, the specific misery of a body that has lain too long on stone that has never once been warm. And he was glad of it. That was the strange thing, the thing he would remember later as the first genuinely his own response he had in this place: he was *grateful* for the cold, the way you are grateful for a hard pinch that wakes you from a dream in which you were drowning.

Because the cold meant skin. And skin meant a body. And a body meant he was not, whatever else was true, a thought adrift in the dark with nothing under it — he was a thing with edges, a thing the world could press against, and the pressing hurt, and the hurt was the most reassuring sensation he had ever had or could remember having.

He tested the having of lungs. He drew a breath in, deliberately, the way you test a sore tooth with your tongue to see if it is really there — and the air came in flat and mineral and cold, and underneath the flatness, faint, there was a sweetness. He disliked it at once. It was not the sweetness of flowers or fruit; it was the sweetness of a thing kept too long, the cloy that rises off something that should have been thrown out and was not, the smell a room gets when something has died in the wall and no one has found it yet. It was very faint. It was everywhere. He understood, without knowing how he understood it, that it was the smell of the place itself, that the whole world smelled like this, and that he would be smelling it for as long as he was here, and that he had better get used to it the way you get used to the smell of your own sickroom.

He breathed it out. The breath fogged.

He watched it go — a small gray ghost of warmth that rose off his lips and hung in the still air a moment and then thinned and was gone, taken by the cold the way everything warm was taken here, he would learn, the way the torchlight would be taken and the lives would be taken and very nearly he himself. But he did not know that yet. What he knew, watching the fog of his own breath rise and vanish, was something simpler and more important.

It was his. The fog was his. He had made it, out of heat he was carrying inside him, heat his own body was generating against the cold — and nothing else, in all the gray reach of ground around him, was making any. He turned his head, slowly, the cold stone grating against his cheek, and looked, and as far as he could see in the dim even light there was no other breath fogging, no other warmth ghosting up into the still air. He was the only thing here that was warm. He did not yet know what that meant. But he felt the loneliness of it before he understood the danger of it, and the loneliness was bad enough.

He sat up.

---

The world resolved by pieces, the way a strange room resolves when you wake in an unfamiliar house and have to assemble where you are out of the dark, one shape at a time.

Ground, first. Directly under his hands: broken stone, frost-furred, cold enough that his palms stuck faintly to it and tore free with a small sting when he lifted them. Not natural stone — paving. A square of it, dressed and laid by hands, once, and then heaved up and cracked across by some force from below, the slabs canted at angles, frost in every seam. A made place. A place that had been built, which meant a place that had had builders, which meant — he turned the thought over slowly, because it had an edge — a place where people had been.

Walls, next, when he lifted his eyes from the ground but kept them carefully below the sky: far off, dark, immense. They ringed the broken square at a distance he couldn't judge, because there was nothing of known size between him and them to judge by. They were simply *there*, a great dark boundary to the world, and he set them aside to think about later, because there was something nearer that his eyes had been avoiding the way they'd avoided the sky.

The shapes. Between him and the far walls, scattered across the heaved paving and the gray ground beyond it, were shapes he had taken at first for more rubble — slumped forms, frost-grayed, the size and lump of fallen masonry. But they were not all rubble. Some of them stood.

He made himself look at the standing ones.

They were people. Or they had been; the word wanted a tense and he could not find the right one. They stood in the postures of a life caught between one second and the next — and not a restful life, not people paused in conversation, but people *interrupted*, frozen in the middle of motion that had meant something urgent. The nearest: a figure stooped low, one arm reaching down and out, the hand cupped and grasping, frozen in the act of lifting something off the ground that was no longer there to lift. Beyond it, another: standing straight but twisted at the waist, one arm flung up across the face, the whole body braced against a blow — and there was no one swinging at it, no blow anywhere, just the figure holding forever its flinch from a thing that had either already landed or never come. And beyond that, more, and more past those, scattered out across the gray to the foot of the far walls: a whole population of the stopped, each one frost-rimed and gray and utterly still, each one held in the exact attitude of the instant their life had been interrupted.

It was not a ruin.

He understood this slowly, and the understanding was worse than the sky had been, because the sky was only frightening and this was something closer to obscene. A ruin is a place where things ended. A ruin is the *aftermath* — the walls fallen, the people gone or dead and decently so, the event complete and receding into the past where events belong. This was not that. Nothing here had been allowed to end. These people had not died; they had been *stopped*, caught mid-reach, mid-flinch, mid-breath, and held that way — and the holding, he understood with a slow cold crawl up the back of his neck, was not a thing that had happened once and finished. The holding was still going on. Right now. The reaching hand was still reaching, this very second, and would be reaching the next second, and the next, forever, the gesture neither completing nor releasing, a life pinned alive to the single worst moment of itself and not permitted to move on.

He got his feet under him.

It took effort. His body was stiff with cold and with whatever long stillness had preceded his waking, and his legs did not entirely trust the ground, and he came up swaying with one hand out for a balance there was nothing to lean on. But he came up. He stood, the only upright thing in that field that had chosen to be upright, the only one whose posture was its own and not a sentence — and he looked out over the stopped people under the bruised sky, and he did not know his own name, but he knew, with the whole cold certainty of the one warm thing in a frozen field, two things.

The first was that he did not belong here.

The second was that everything here would, sooner or later, notice that he was warm.

---

"You're up. Good. I wasn't sure you would be."

The voice came from no direction.

He spun — too fast, his cold legs nearly went out from under him — turning all the way around, because the voice had been *right there*, clear and close and level, a woman's voice speaking to him from a distance of no more than a few feet, and there was no one. The stopped figures stood in their frost. The broken paving lay empty. The far walls held their distance. He turned again, slower, scanning the gray ground at the level a crouching person would be, behind the canted slabs, anywhere a body could be — and there was nothing, no one, and the voice had not come from the air either, he realized, because the still air carried nothing; he had already learned that, learned it from his own fog rising silent and from the absolute soundlessness of the field. The voice had not traveled to him through anything. It had simply *been there*, inside the place where his own thoughts were, except that it was not his thought — it was even, and unhurried, and faintly, dryly amused, and it belonged unmistakably to someone who was paying him a calm and complete attention, and had been, he understood with a fresh crawl of unease, for some time before he woke.

"There's no one to look at," the voice said, and there was the amusement again, gentle, not unkind, the patience of someone watching an expected thing happen. "You'll wear yourself out. I'm not in there with you. I can't be — if I came in, the place would break, and you'd break along with it, and then all of this would have been a waste of a great deal of effort. So you'll have to make do with hearing me. I know it's strange. You'll get used to it faster than you'd think. People do."

"Where—" he started, and stopped.

His voice came out as a scrape, a raw ugly croak from a throat that felt as though it hadn't been used in a very long time — perhaps it hadn't; perhaps it never had, he had no memory of ever speaking — and the smallness of it shamed him obscurely, and besides, *where* was only the first of far too many words, and he did not know which one to spend the second of his life's speech on. Where. What. How. Who. Why. They crowded up behind the one scraped syllable and jammed there.

"Where, what, how, who," the voice said, as if it had heard the jam and was unknotting it for him. "Yes. All of those. In about that order, usually." A pause — not a hesitation, he would come to learn she did not hesitate, but a choosing, the deliberate pause of someone deciding how much to hand over and in what sequence so that it would land cleanly the first time. "I'll give you the short version, because the long one won't help you stand up any straighter and we genuinely don't have the time for it. You're inside a piece of a world that died. It broke off when it died, and it's been sitting somewhere very bad ever since, and the very bad somewhere has been slowly turning it into more of itself. That's the sky you've been so carefully not looking at. You were right not to, by the way. Keep not doing it. It's the single most dangerous thing in here, and the danger isn't that it'll fall on you. It's that it'll get into your thinking. So. Eyes down. Good."

He kept his eyes down. He looked at the cracked stone between his feet, at the frost in the seams, at the small fog of his own breath going up and being eaten. It steadied him to be told what to do. He noticed that, too, and filed it with the rest: that in the absence of everything else, a clear instruction in a level voice was a thing to hold onto.

"And me," he said. The scrape was a little better the second time. "What about me. If everything here is — stopped. What am I."

"You," the voice said, "are the part I didn't expect."

And for the first time something moved under the evenness — not warmth, exactly, he would have distrusted warmth, but a small sharpening, the quickening of interest in a person who has been performing a long and entirely routine task and has just turned up, in the middle of it, the one thing that does not fit the established pattern.

"Everything in here is dead," she said. "It's been dead a very long time, longer than you have a frame for, and the trouble with it — the whole trouble, the reason it's such a mess — is that none of it is allowed to *finish* being dead. It can't end. It just goes on, stopped, the way you've seen. That's the rule of this place. Nothing ends." A beat. "And then there's you. Sitting up. Breathing. Making heat, making fog, making that frightened face — a living man, warm all the way through, exactly where there is no possible reason for a living warm man to be. Do you see why I find you interesting? I have been doing this a long time. I am not often handed something I can't account for. You are, at the moment, the only thing in this entire dead world that I cannot explain, and I would very much like to."

"How," he managed. "How am I— if everything's dead, how am I—"

"That," the voice said, "is an extremely good question. It's the same one I have. I don't have the answer to it yet, and I want one, and that is the first of several reasons that you and I are going to come to an arrangement."

---

The arrangement came out of her in plain pieces, one at a time, and the strangest part of it — stranger than the voice, stranger almost than the sky — was that he believed every piece as it landed. He had no reason to. He knew nothing about her, not her name, not her face, not whether she meant him well or ill. But she said each thing in the tone of someone who has never once in her existence needed to lie, because the plain truth has always been sufficient to her purposes, and the tone got past his guard in a way that argument never could have.

The piece of dead world was drifting, she told him. Slowly, through the bad place it was sealed in, toward somewhere she lived — somewhere she was responsible for. And if it arrived there, if it came all the way and touched what she guarded, it would tear something open that she would not be able to reach in time to close, and a great many living things, real ones, warm ones like him, would pay for that with their lives. So the piece could not be allowed to arrive. It had to be dealt with first. That was the clock, she said, and she did not dress it up, did not tell him how long, did not pretend the deadline was generous or the danger small.

"I won't give you a number," she said, when he asked. "I can't give you one I'd stand behind. The drift isn't even — it speeds and it slows, and any number I named would either make you reckless or make you despair, and both of those get a man killed in here. So instead of a number I'll give you the truth, which is this: long enough to do the thing properly, if you don't waste it, and not long enough to do it twice. You understand the difference? You get one attempt. There's no second descent. So we don't hurry, and we don't dawdle, and we don't lie to ourselves about which one we're doing."

He noticed the *we*. He set it aside.

But the piece could not simply be destroyed, she went on — and here the second half of it came, the half that needed him. Because the dead world was the last of its kind. A whole world, unique, unrepeatable, one that no one living had ever seen or ever would again, and she did not burn the last copy of a thing without reading it first. She *kept* what was ending. That was hers; that was the work she did; and it meant the fragment had to be gone *into*, and seen, and understood, before it could be allowed to end — and that part she could not do herself. She would shatter it if she entered; she was too large, too heavy, the wrong kind of thing. And the dead could not do it, because the dead saw nothing anymore, were nothing now but the stopped gesture and the gray frost. It needed something that was alive, and small enough to walk in the stopped place without breaking it, and awake enough to look at what was there and *understand* it.

It needed him.

"So here is the arrangement," she said. "You go down. This world has a shape — it goes down, in layers, from this broken square at the top all the way to the thing at the bottom that started the dying. You go down through it. You look at what's in each layer, and you don't just survive it, you *understand* it — really understand it, all the way to the part that's hard to look at — and when the whole of it has been read, it can be ended. Cleanly. All of it at once, the way it should have ended an age ago. And your way out comes with the ending. That's the deal. You do the looking. I'll handle the rest — I'll lend you what you need to survive the going, and I'll keep off your back the things that would otherwise take an interest. You read the dead world. I get you out the far side of it. Both of us get what we need."

"And if I say no," he said.

He didn't mean it as defiance. He genuinely wanted to know the shape of the alternative; it was the inventory-taking thing in him, the part that wanted all the exits mapped before it agreed to walk through any of them.

"Then you stay here," she said. No threat in it. That was what made it land — she delivered it as flatly as she'd delivered everything else, a fact rather than a leverage. "Alive, in a place that turns living things into furniture, for as long as you can hold out. And you'll hold out a while — you're stubborn, I can already see that — but the place is patient and you are not immortal, and one day it stops being able to tell the difference between you and the rest of the stopped, and then there's one more gray figure in the square, reaching for something that isn't there." A pause. "I'm not going to make you. It genuinely doesn't work if I make you — that's not me being delicate, it's a fact about the work, you have to choose it or it doesn't take. But I'd think hard about how few of your available futures end with you standing under a sky that isn't that one."

He looked up — not at the sky, he kept his eyes off the sky — but out, at the far dark walls, and the stopped figures, and the small steady fog of his own breath rising and being eaten by the cold. The only warm thing for a dead world in every direction.

"You said the rest," he said. "You'll handle the rest. What's the rest. What are you lending me."

---

"Mana," she said. "Power, if you like the plainer word. You can't do any of this on a mortal's own fuel — a living body has just enough in it to run a living body, and not a drop spare for the things you're going to need to do. So I'll lend you mine. You'll be able to do things you have no business being able to do: push, when pushing is needed. Mend. Make a light. Hold a thing shut that wants to come open. Within reason." A pause, and the voice changed — not louder, but more deliberate, the way a person leans in slightly to say the one thing they most need you to retain. "And here is the rule that goes with it, the one rule I need you holding from the very first hour, because it's the one that has killed more of the people I've sent into places like this than anything the places themselves contain. Not too much. Not too fast."

"Why," he said.

"Because there are things outside this fragment. Out in the bad place it's sealed in. Large things — larger than you have any framework for; I won't try to give you one, you'd only get it wrong and small. They don't know you're in here. The only reason they don't is that I am standing between them and this place, and they have no particular reason to look closer. A small spending of power, they don't notice — it's a candle in a dark field, beneath their attention. But a large spending is a *bonfire*. It's a reason to turn and look. And I can hold off a very great deal, but I would strongly prefer not to discover exactly how much, with you standing at the bottom of the thing they'd be looking at." The voice settled. "So. Spend small, and they keep not looking. Spend big, and you put a light in the window, and something out there in the dark turns its head. Do you understand me? This is the rule. The rest is negotiable. This one isn't."

"Spend small," he said. "Don't put a light in the window."

"Good." Something that was almost, not quite, a thread of dry approval. "You listen properly. You take the thing as said instead of arguing it down to the size you'd prefer it to be. That's twice now you've done something I didn't expect — first by waking, and now by not wasting my breath. Most people argue with the part where I tell them not to use the gift I've just finished giving them. They want to know the exact size of *too much*. They want the edge mapped so they can walk right up to it. You just took it and put it away." A beat. "Keep being like that. It's rare, and it's most of what keeps the rare ones alive."

He didn't argue. He was, in fact, no longer entirely listening, because something else had taken his attention — the simple enormous fact of his own situation had arrived in him all at once, the way the cold had, from the edges in. He was standing. He was breathing. He was *cold*, and aching, and afraid, in the middle of a field of things that would never again do any of those, and the only road out of the field ran down, into the dark layers of a dead world, toward a thing at the bottom that had killed it. He was afraid. He made himself notice the fear, the way he'd made himself notice the cold, and the noticing turned it, again, into proof: the stopped figures were not afraid of anything. Fear was a thing you could only do if you still had something left to lose. He was the only one in the field who had anything left to lose, and the fear was the receipt for it.

And then the figure turned.

The nearest one — the standing one, the one with its arm flung up across its face against a blow that had never landed — was facing him. And he was almost certain, with the cold certainty that had become his only instrument, that a moment ago it had been facing the other way.

His heart went hard and quick in his chest. And the field — he felt it rather than saw it — the field seemed to *lean*. All that stopped gray attention, scattered across the broken square and out to the walls, tilted toward him by some fraction of a degree, the way a room of standing grass tilts toward a single warmth, the way water leans toward the low place in a floor. It was not fast. There was nothing fast in it. But it was a turning-toward, a whole dead field beginning, without hurry and without thought, to orient on the one warm moving thing in it — and the animal in him screamed *run*, screamed it with everything it had, and the other part of him, the cold clear part that had been listening to the voice, said, with equal force: *no.*

Because he understood — without being told, with a certainty that startled him by how completely it was his own — that if he ran now, in the first minute, from the first dead thing that turned toward him, then he would run from the second, and the third, and every one after that, and the running would become the thing he was, and the running would be what finished him, not the dead. The dead were slow. Fear was fast, and fear was the real predator here, and fear would chase him in circles around a dead world until he dropped.

So he made himself a rule.

The first one. He built it deliberately, on purpose, with the clear cold part of his mind, the way a man drives a single stake into ground that will not hold anything else — because he understood, standing there, a thing about the place that the voice had not told him and did not need to: that this was a world built to *erase* what he was, to wear the warm specific frightened self out of him and fold what was left into the gray, and that if he meant to stay himself inside it he was going to have to *build* himself, piece by piece, out of his own choices, because nothing here was going to be given to him and everything here was going to be taken. And the pieces had better be his own. They had better be things he chose and could name and could hold, because they were going to be the only walls he had.

*Don't freeze.*

That was the first stake. He drove it in. He held still — not the frozen stillness of the field, but a chosen stillness, a held breath — until his heart came down out of his throat and his breathing slowed and the scream of the animal quieted to a manageable noise. And then he looked the turned figure full in its frost-blank face, the reaching arm, the flung-up guard, the gray nothing where its eyes had been, and he did not run.

"There he is," the voice said.

It was quiet now. Watchful. And it gave away nothing at all about how much of this — the waking, the turning figure, the rule driven in under pressure — it might have arranged, or foreseen, or hoped for.

"All right," she said. "Let's begin. Walk toward the wall — the near one, where the ground falls away; there's a stair down, you'll find it. And keep your hands where you can see them. Not for my sake. For yours. You'll understand the first time the ground does something you don't expect, and I'd rather you were already in the habit."

He took a breath of the flat, sweet, dead air. He looked once more at the figure that had turned, and the field that leaned, and the bruised lid of the sky he would not look at. Five things he knew about himself now, he thought, counting them like a man counting coins in the dark: that he was alive; that he was warm; that he was afraid; that he did not belong here; and that he did not run.

It was not much to be. It was more than he'd had when he woke.

He took a step.

The fog of his breath went up behind him, the only warm thing for a dead world in every direction — and somewhere far past the bruised lid of the sky, in the dark between the worlds, something the size of a city did not, yet, turn to look.
