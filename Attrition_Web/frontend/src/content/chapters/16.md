# Ch16 · The Choice

He thought for a long time, in the violet quiet, and the thinking was the hardest work he had done in a place that had been nothing but hard work from the first breath.

He thought about the second door first, because it was the one he wanted, and he had a rule about not lying to himself and that meant looking straight at the thing he wanted before he did anything else with it. Walking out. Living. A real sky — a true one, not the bruise. He let himself want it, all the way, the way he'd let himself feel the cold on the first day and the warm water in Talwyn's ward: honestly, without flinching from the size of it. And the size of it was enormous. To be *done.* To be a man who had survived a terrible thing and gotten to set it down and walk out and go live an ordinary life somewhere warm, somewhere the sky was only the sky and asked nothing of him and held nothing behind it. He had only just gotten his life back — woken into it under the bruise, the one warm thing in a dead world, and learned across an age-long descent how to keep it — and the thought of spending it, at last, on his own behalf, of being allowed to, was so sweet it ached in his chest like a second wound. No one would fault him. Iris had said so, plainly, and she didn't lie to the ones who'd stopped surprising her. *Walk out. Live. You've more than earned it.* It was true. It was simply, wholly true.

And he thought about the first door, because *don't lie to yourself* did not get suspended for the comfortable parts, and the first door was the least comfortable thing there was. His left arm was gone to the shoulder now — quiet, deathless, useful, *not his* — and he could feel the rot at the bottom of the world working at the rest of him, patient, strong here at its source, and he understood with a cold clarity that the first door was not a thing that *might* happen to him. It was a thing that was already happening. An inch at a time. And all it needed, to finish, was for him to get tired enough, at the wrong moment, to stop forcing the hurt back in. He did not have to imagine where that ended. It was waiting for him at the edge of the throne-light right now, calm and patient and wearing a face — the Fifth, the thing on the stair, the one who *had* gotten tired enough, once, and set the weight down, and never picked it up again. The first door was not an abstraction here. It was a person he could become, standing a few feet away, who had already taken from him the one thing he most wanted to carry. He had only to stop, the way it had stopped. And the most frightening thing Iris had said in the whole descent was also the truest, and it had a body now: *that one is close. Closer than you think.*

And under both doors, in everything he weighed, sat the boy. The hole where the boy had been. He had failed already — the great clean run of his competence was broken, and a small wrong-handed soul he'd promised to a dead father was gone into the hunger because Ren had been proud and hadn't counted the room. He could have let that stop him. He noticed the pull of it: the way a fresh failure wants to become a reason to quit, to say *I am not the one after all, I lose them, look, I lost him.* But Maren had given him back his own medicine on the stone — *you failed at the impossible thing and did the real thing anyway; hold both* — and so he held both, the boy he lost and the four he carried, and did not let the losing cast the deciding vote. That, too, was new in him. A day ago he had never failed and so had never had to learn this. Now he had, and had.

---

And then he thought about the third door. The work. Iris's work — with hands inside it, at last.

He turned it over honestly, looking for the lie in it, because a thing offered by a god who had admitted, freely, to managing people deserved to be checked for the hook. And he found, turning it over and over in the violet quiet, that there wasn't a hook — or rather, that Iris had shown him the hook herself, named it, held it up to the light with her own hand: *a life sentence to other people's endings, an eternity of grief that is never your own.* She hadn't sold it to him. She had done the opposite of selling it. She had told him it might be the cruelest thing she had ever offered anyone, and she had meant it, and he believed her, because she did not lie to the ones who'd stopped surprising her and he had long since stopped.

But he kept coming back to one thing, and it would not go away, and it was the thing the whole descent had built in him, rule by rule, the way he'd built himself out of nothing in a place designed to erase him.

Somewhere, right now, another world was dying.

Dissolving into the Void, unwitnessed, uncarried — every Karr of it, every Talwyn, every small laughing woman and every uncounted clerk, going down into nothing, unmade, forgotten, with no one walking down through its strata to tell the soldier he could stand down, or the healer she could finally grieve, or the priest that his one true miracle had been real. Iris stood at the breach and kept the dark off it and carried out the *shape* of it — the obituary, the cover of a book no one would ever open — and it was not enough, she had said so, it had never once in an age been enough, because no one could go *in.*

He could go in. He was, perhaps, the only one who could. He had just proven it — five times, four freed and one woman at the bottom, down the whole length of a dead world.

And the thing he kept arriving at, underneath the exhaustion and the ache and the unbearable sweetness of the second door, was very simple, and it would not let him go: *if I don't, who does? If not the one who can — then no one. And the worlds go down into the dark uncarried, forever.* It was the same arithmetic as the cistern, the first day, scaled up now past the edge of his own single life. There was one road that answered the thing that actually needed answering. The other two roads were about *him* — about his rest, his life, his earned and aching wish to be done. This one was about everyone, in every world, who would ever otherwise die forgotten in the dark.

He did not want it. He wanted the second door. He noticed that, clearly, and did not lie to himself about it, and chose the third anyway — and understood, choosing it, that the not-wanting was the thing that made the choice worth anything at all. A yes from a man with nowhere else to go is just surrender wearing a brave face. He had somewhere else to go. He had the best somewhere-else there was. And he was going to turn from it, with his eyes open, on purpose, because the work was real and only he could do it.

"You've decided," Iris said quietly. It was not a question. "I can hear it. You went still in a particular way — the way you went still on the square, the first hour, before you made the first rule."

"I want the second door," Ren said. "I need you to know that. I want to walk out and live and let the sky just be the sky. I'm not choosing past it because I don't want it. I want it more than I've wanted anything since I woke."

"I know," Iris said. "That is the only thing that would make your yes worth having. A yes from someone who didn't want the other thing is just a man with nowhere to go, dressed up as a hero. You have somewhere to go. You have the best somewhere I could ever offer. And you're turning from it anyway. That's why I'll believe it. That's the only reason I'd let you choose it at all."

---

Ren opened his eyes and turned to Maren, who had held the door an age and one moment longer.

"You can let it fall now," he said. "The seal. I've walked all the way up through your people, and all the way back down to you, and I'm telling you — as the last living child of Eldravir, the one with the most right of anyone in any world to hold this against you — let it fall. You held the door until the right hands came. The right hands are here. It's done, Maren. You can set it down. You didn't fail them. You kept them whole until someone could carry them home, and that someone has come, and he's going to carry them. All of them. So you can let go."

And Maren — the Crown, the one who decided, who had sat an age at the bottom of her single terrible choice unable to learn whether she had damned her people or saved them — looked at the living child who was all that was left of the people she had sealed, and found in his face the one thing she had needed and could never once give herself. Not absolution. Not condemnation. *Permission.* Permission to have failed at the impossible thing and succeeded, completely, at the real one. Permission to stop being the woman who decided, and become, at the very last, only a tired person who was allowed, finally, to rest.

"Thank you," she said. It came out of her like a breath she had held for an age and was at last permitted to let go. "I have wanted, for so long, for someone to come and *know* — and not hate me for it, and not forgive me for it either, neither of those, just — know, the whole of it, and let me put it down." Her smooth worn eyes filled, the first feeling he had seen move in them. "Carry them well, living one. Carry all of them. We were a good people, before the sky came down. We were worth the keeping. Tell whoever you carry us to, wherever that is — tell them we were worth keeping."

"You were," Ren said. "I'll tell them. I promise. And I don't lie — I've got a rule about it."

Maren smiled — small, and worn, and at peace — and she lifted her folded hands, the same hands that had raised the seal in the last hour of the world, and she let it fall.

---

The ending came up through the fragment like a tide coming home.

It was not violent. That was the mercy of it, after all of it — the seal did not shatter, it *released*, an age of held breath finally, gently exhaled, and Ren felt it move outward and upward from the throne-room through the whole of the dead world at once. The rot stopped, because there was nothing left to rot. The half-death finished. Far above him, he knew without seeing, the frozen square was unfreezing into rest — the woman with the child completing at last the step she'd been stopped on an age ago, and going, gently, with her child, into a dark that was finally allowed to be only the dark. Karr's parade ground. Talwyn's eased ward. Bran's quiet cathedral. Tomas's emptied shelves. All of it, letting go, all at once, the way a single long-held note finally resolves and the silence that comes after is not loss but completion.

And Maren, on her low dark seat, went still — not stopped-still, not frozen, but the deep and complete stillness of a person who has reached the end of an unbearable task and been allowed, at the very last, to lay it down. The last of the five. At rest.

The violet began to thin.

"It's ending," Iris said, and her voice was gentle now, gentle and urgent at once. "All of it, the way it should have an age ago. Clean. No rupture. No collision. You did the thing I needed done, Ren — you read it, all the way down, and you ended it *true.*" A pause, the urgency sharpening to an edge. "But it's ending *now*, and you're still inside it, and the ending does not care which door you chose. So whatever you've decided — you have to *move.* Because the only door that closes on its own, the only one that happens if you simply stay where you are and let the tide take you, is the first one. Get up. The fragment is dissolving, and you are the one warm living thing still inside it, and it's time. Up, Ren. Up the strata. Carry them out. *Go.*"

Ren got to his feet in the thinning violet, at the bottom of his dead and finally-resting world, the whole of it lifting away around him into the dark like breath off a cooling field — every soul he had freed, every soul he had promised to carry — and his ruined arm hung quiet at his side and his living heart beat hard and afraid and *his*, and far above him, impossibly far up through the unmaking strata, there was the faintest first thread of a light that was not violet, not the bruise, not the Void.

A real sky, waiting at the top of the world. And past it, the breach, and the dark between worlds, and the work.

He took a breath of the failing air. He had chosen. And he began to climb.
