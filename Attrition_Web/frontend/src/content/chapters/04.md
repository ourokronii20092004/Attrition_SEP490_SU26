# Ch04 · The Wall

The tunnel let out onto a parade ground, and the parade ground was full, and the parade ground was the first thing in Eldravir that was not stopped.

It moved.

That was the horror of it — that here, at last, after the frozen square and the leaning lanes and the folded sleepers in the dry basins, something *moved*, and the moving was worse, immeasurably worse, than all the stillness had been. He came out of the mouth of the tunnel into a great flat yard at the foot of an inner wall, and rank on rank of the gray dead stood across it in formation, and they were *drilling*. Spears rose and fell in something that reached for unison and almost found it. Gray feet stamped a quarter-turn, ragged, a half-beat scattered across the line but a turn, a real coordinated turn. A row of them advanced four paces and set their shields with a clack of old wood and held the line, and held it, and held it. And up and down the front of the formation walked a single figure who was not gray — who was *dark*, a great broad slab of a shape, taller than the tallest of the dead by a full head, and from it came the voice he'd heard down the tunnel, calling the count, rolling out across the yard like a thing dragged over gravel: *and one, and two, and set, and hold — hold — hold —*

"There he is," Iris said, very quiet, very close. "Don't go out there yet. Stay in the mouth of the tunnel, in the dark, and watch. You learn a man fastest by watching him do the thing he can't stop doing, before he knows he's being watched."

Ren stayed in the dark of the tunnel mouth, and watched.

---

He had expected, when Iris said *a dead commander drilling dead troops*, something mechanical — a recording stuck in its groove, a thing repeating because repeating was all that was left of it, the count called by rote into the faces of soldiers who no longer registered it. That was not what he saw. What he saw was worse, and it took him a while to understand why it was worse, and the understanding sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

Karr *worked* them. He saw them. He was not calling the count into the air; he was calling it at specific soldiers, watching each one, and when a spear came up late — when one gray figure in the third rank brought its weapon to the angle a half-beat behind the rest — Karr was *there*, at that soldier's shoulder, before the beat had finished, his big dark hands closing over the gray hands on the haft, lifting them, setting the angle, holding them there while he said something low that Ren couldn't hear from the tunnel but could read in the shape of it, the bend of the great head down to the small gray one: *like this. You've done it a thousand times. Do it once more, right.* When a shield dropped, he set it back up. When a line bowed in the middle, he walked its whole length and pressed it straight with the flat of one broad hand, soldier by soldier by soldier, patient past any patience Ren had a word for, more patient than stone, more patient than the cold.

He was not a tyrant grinding the dead through their paces. He was a teacher who loved his students and could not be made to understand that the lesson was over — that there was no examination coming, no battle, no use that the drill was drilling toward. He worked them the way a good man works at a thing he believes will save the people he's responsible for. And that — a teacher's love, poured out forever into a purpose that no longer existed — was so much worse than cruelty that Ren felt it climb his throat.

"He was the Shield," Iris said. "When the world was alive, and the thing came down out of the sky onto it, there were five who stood up. Five who became what a people leans its whole weight on when everything else is going. The histories — your histories, the ones that burned with everything else — they'd have called them the five pillars. Each of them was what a civilization needs in its worst hour, made into a person. And Karr was the one they leaned on to *hold*. To make the line that doesn't break. To stand in the gap so the others could do whatever the others did." A pause. "And he did it. That's the thing you have to hold onto while you watch him, because you're going to want to pity him and pity isn't the truth of him. He *succeeded*. He made a line that held — long enough for some of them to get out. Not many. But some. The ones who reached the gate you walked past in the lane above, the ones who were trying so hard to get through it. They got through it because Karr Drennan stood at this wall and would not let it break, and people are alive somewhere — were alive, once, for a while, in whatever time the world had left — because of what this man did here. He held. People lived because he held. And then the world ended around him, mid-hold, and the holding was never allowed to stop."

"They got out," Ren said, quietly, watching the great dark figure set a gray soldier's shield back into the line. "He won. The thing he was for — he did it. It worked."

"He did it. Yes."

"Then why is he still—"

"Because no one ever came and told him he could stop."

The voice was even, but it was the evenness of a thing being held level by main force, the way you hold a full cup level crossing a room.

"Think about what an ending actually requires," Iris said. "Not just the event — the *acknowledgment*. A battle doesn't end when the last blow falls; it ends when someone with the authority to say so says *it's over, stand down, you held, you can put it down now.* The horn. The officer walking up the line. The word. That's what lets a soldier stop being a soldier and go back to being a man. And Karr never got it. There was no after for him. The world ended mid-battle — froze him in the single second his entire soul was organized around, the holding, the gap, the line — and there was no one left with the authority or the breath to sound the stand-down. So he holds. He has held for an age. He drills the dead who held beside him, gets them ready, keeps them sharp, for an assault that already came and that he already survived and won — because the only alternative to getting them ready is to admit there is nothing left to get them ready for, and a man who has made himself entirely into a shield does not know how to be anything at all in a world with nothing left to guard. The holding is the last of him. If he stops holding, he stops. And no one has ever given him permission to stop."

Ren watched Karr come back down the line, again, to a soldier near the end of the third rank — a small one, he saw now, shorter than the others by a head, a young one, a boy almost — and close his great hands over the boy's small ones on the spear-haft, and lift them to the angle, and hold them there.

---

"How do I get past him," Ren said.

"That," Iris said, "is the wrong question. And I want you to feel why it's wrong before I let you ask the right one, because the wrongness is the whole lesson of this stratum and if you don't get it here you'll die at the next. You're thinking of him as a door. You want the trick that opens the door — the password, the weak point, the lever. There's no trick. He isn't a door. He's a man with exactly one idea left in him, and you do not get *past* a man like that, you—"

She did not finish, because Karr had seen him.

The count stopped. Not trailed off — *stopped*, cut clean, mid-syllable, and the whole gray field of the formation froze with it, spears caught at odd half-raised heights, the silence rushing in so suddenly that Ren heard his own heartbeat in it. The great dark head had come up and around, and the eyes in it had found him — found the warm living shape in the mouth of the tunnel the way a lamp swung in a dark room finds the one face in it — and then Karr was crossing the yard toward him.

He did not run. His pace was not a run. But it ate the distance like one, a long ground-covering stride that brought the enormous dark shape across the whole width of the parade ground in the time it took Ren's body to scream the old scream — *run, run, the thing has seen you, run* — and Ren set his three rules against the scream like stakes driven into a flood, and held.

*Don't freeze.*

He stepped out of the tunnel mouth. Out of the dark, into the dim violet of the yard, onto the broken edge of the parade ground, and he let the dead man come to him standing — chin level, hands at his sides, not reaching for the wall, not backing toward the tunnel, just standing, the way he'd stood and faced the turned figure in the square.

Up close, Karr was vast. And he was not, Ren saw, entirely a man — the build was too heavy through the shoulders and the jaw, the brow too broad and shelved, two short tusks set at the corners of a wide mouth, the gray skin stretched over all of it cracked like a dry riverbed in summer. Some kind of orc, or what the word had been in a world Ren had never gotten the chance to know. And his eyes — his eyes were the only part of him that hadn't gone gray. They were a living brown, warm, terribly warm, the warm of a man glad down to the floor of his soul to see you, and that warmth fixed on Ren and *read* him, head to foot, the way Karr read a soldier's stance in the line — searching, Ren understood with a cold lurch, for the thing that needed correcting. For the flinch. For the place where the man in front of him was soft, and might break, and could be made hard enough to live.

"You're warm," Karr said.

The gravel voice, up close, had a crack running through it — wonder, raw and disbelieving. "By every god that's still listening. You're *warm.* A live one. After all this time, they've sent me a live one." He looked Ren over again, slower, and his cracked gray face did something complicated and terrible — hope, naked undefended hope, the hope of a man who has been short-handed on a wall for an age and has prayed, in whatever way a thing like him prays, for exactly this. "Can you hold a spear, son? Tell me you can hold a spear. Tell me you can stand in a line and not break when it comes. It's coming — it's been coming a long while, and the men are tired, I won't lie to you, they're so tired, but it's *coming* — and I am so short of anyone who can still feel their own hands."

And Ren — who had been warned, twice, who knew precisely what this was, who had three rules driven into the ground of himself and a clear cold understanding of the trap — Ren looked into the warm desperate brown eyes of a man who had saved everyone he could and been denied, forever, the one word that would have let him know it, and found that he did not, in that moment, have it in him to say the true thing. *There is no assault. You're dead. They're all dead. The war you're drilling for ended an age before I was born, and you won it.* It was true, and it was right there, and he could not make his mouth deliver it into that hoping face.

"I can hold a spear," he heard himself say instead.

"*Ren,*" said Iris in his ear — not a warning, not sharp. Just his name. Quiet. Marking the place where he had stepped off the road she'd told him to keep to, the way you'd note a stone you'd tripped on so you'd remember it on the way back.

Karr's whole cracked gray face broke open into a grin of pure, undefended relief, and he clapped a hand down on Ren's shoulder — a hand the size of a dinner plate, that nearly drove Ren to his knees — and it was warm, the hand was *warm*, leaning toward Ren's heat the way all of them leaned, but with a will behind it, a whole grieving person behind it, and he turned Ren toward the waiting gray ranks the way a father turns a son to show him the land he'll one day work.

"Then fall in," Karr said. "Fall in, and I'll make you ready before it comes. I make everyone ready. That's the whole of what I'm for."

And Ren walked out onto the parade ground, in among the ranks of the gray, and did not yet know how he was going to get out of it — only that the one way out that was definitely closed to him was the way the scream had wanted, the fight, the burst of borrowed power, because the leash sat cold and certain in his chest, *spend small, don't put a light in the window*, and there was no quantity of *small* that fought free of a standing army, and a *large* would only bring something through the bruised roof of the sky to look. He would have to think. He fell in at the end of the third rank, beside the small one, the young one, the boy who held his spear with a white-knuckled wrongness that even Ren could see, an angle so plainly off that he understood it was going to be corrected the moment Karr came back down the line — and he set his feet the way the gray soldiers set theirs, and he waited for the count, and he began, the way he began everything now, to look for the crack that no one had accounted for.

"All right," Iris said, very low. "You didn't take my advice. We'll talk about that, too, when there's time for the talking. But you're in the line now, so — watch the boy beside you. I think you've already noticed him; I think that's half of why your mouth said yes instead of the truth. Watch how Karr is with that one, specifically. Watch it closely." A pause, and something in it he couldn't read, something held very carefully level. "That's the thread, Ren. Pull it gently. It's going to be the worst thing you've seen yet, and it's the way out."
