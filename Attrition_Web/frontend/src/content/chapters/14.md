# Ch14 · The Throne

For a long time Ren did not say anything, because the thing in his chest was too large to speak around, and because for the first time in the whole descent the person he had to free was a person who had done something *to him* — to the child he had been, to the parents he could not remember, to every soul he had been born among.

He could feel the easy answers crowding up in him, hot and available.

*You damned them.* That one was right there, and it had teeth. He could stand here as the last living child of Eldravir and tell the small tired woman on the low dark seat that she had been wrong — that she should have let the Void take them, that an eternity of dissolution would have been kinder than the age of rotting half-death she'd sealed them into, that the frozen square where he woke was her doing and her crime. It would even be a kind of truth. It would certainly be a kind of revenge, and some part of him, some orphaned animal part that had woken alone in a dead world, wanted it.

And the other easy answer, its opposite, the kind one, the lie: *you saved them. You did right. Rest easy.* He could give her that. It would cost him nothing and her everything, and it would be a lie — because he didn't know whether she'd done right, *nobody* could know, she'd said so herself and she'd had an age to decide and couldn't — and *don't lie to be kind* meant the comfortable absolution was as closed to him as the cruel condemnation. He had spent the whole descent learning that the comforting lie was just a slower cruelty. He could not hand her one now, even out of pity, even though pity was rising in him like water.

"Careful," Iris said, softly. "You feel both of them pulling. The condemnation and the absolution — the two things every single person who has ever reached this bottom has wanted to say to the one who made the choice. She is braced for both. She has had an age to brace for both. And neither of them is the thing she needs, which is exactly why she's still sitting here and the others above her are at rest. You found the third thing for Bran. You'll have to find it here, too. Take your time. There's no one to drill you down here, no one to keep you, no congregation to lose. There's no clock at the bottom of the world but the one outside — and that one, Ren, I will hold as long as I am able to hold anything. Take the time. This is the one that matters most."

---

So Ren took the time. He sat down — on the cold foundation-stone, across from the low dark seat, the way you sit *with* someone rather than stand over them — and he thought about it the way he had learned to think about everything down here. Not *how do I get past her.* But: *what is she actually stuck on.*

And he saw, slowly, that Iris was right, and that he'd been about to make the same mistake everyone before him had made. Maren wasn't stuck on not knowing whether she'd done right. She had told him herself — *I have wondered every hour since.* She had lived inside that unanswerable question for an age and made a kind of grim peace with never answering it. That wasn't the trap. A person can live a long time inside a question they know they'll never close.

What she could not live inside — what had frozen her here at the bottom while the others at least had their drilling and their healing and their singing to keep their hands busy — was something narrower, and worse. She had no function left. The Shield could still shield, past all sense; the Hearth could still heal, past all mercy. They were at least *doing* the thing they were. But Maren's function had been to decide — and she had decided, once, completely, and there would never be anything left to decide again. She was a ruler with exactly one act in the whole of her reign, and the act was *this*, and she could neither undo it nor repeat it nor move past it nor set it down. She could only go on *being* the person who had made it. Forever. Alone. At the bottom.

"You won't lift the seal," Ren said slowly. "You could. You're the one who raised it — you could let it fall, let them all finish dying, end the whole thing. You've thought about it. Every hour, you said. For an age. So why haven't you?"

Maren was quiet for a moment.

"Because lifting it means I was wrong," she said. "If I let the seal fall now — if I let them all end — then I admit the keeping was never worth it. That I held my whole people in a rotting half-death for an age for *nothing*. That I should have let the Void take them cleanly at the start and spared them all of this. That every moment since the hour I raised it has been a cruelty I inflicted because I could not bear the alternative. To lift the seal is to confess that the single act of my entire reign, the one thing I was ever called upon to decide, was the worst thing anyone in Eldravir ever did to anyone." Her smooth worn eyes came up to his. "And I am not strong enough to confess that and then go on existing. So I don't lift it. I sit here. And it rots. And I tell myself that as long as I haven't lifted it, the question is still open — that I might still, somehow, turn out to have been right." A terrible pause. "You see it, don't you. I'm doing the very thing you came down here to find in all of us. I'm keeping the choice unfinished so that I never have to learn I failed. I am exactly the same as the Flame. I have only got a larger candle, and a colder one."

---

"No," Ren said. "Not the same. And the difference is the whole thing — it's the thing I came all the way down here to give you, I just didn't know its shape until now."

He found, as he started to say it, that the third thing was already in him — had been gathering the whole way down, a piece of it from each of them, Karr and Talwyn and Bran, and here at the bottom it came together into one.

"The Flame was keeping a lie," he said. "You're keeping a *question.* And those aren't the same, because the question is real. You genuinely don't know if you did right — and neither do I, and neither, I think, does the god in my ear who's older than my whole world. Nobody knows. It might not be knowable by anyone. You faced two unbearable doors, with hours to choose and no good option behind either of them, and you chose the one that kept your people *themselves* — and whether that was mercy or cruelty is a question I don't believe has an answer, and I'm not going to insult you by pretending I can hand you one. I can't tell you that you were right. I won't tell you that you were wrong. I don't know. No one does."

He leaned toward her.

"But here is what I *can* tell you, because I'm the only one who can. I'm the child who slipped the edge of your seal. I'm what's left of the people you decided for — the one person in any world with the standing to hold this against you. And I've spent this whole descent walking up through them, Maren, through your sealed people, one stratum at a time. And they were *savable.* Do you understand what I'm telling you? Karr got to put down his war. Talwyn got to grieve her daughter and give back the ones she'd kept. Bran got to stop being right and just be held by his flock. The people you sealed — they were still *themselves* enough to be reached, and freed, and given a true ending, an age after you saved them. If you had let the Void take them at the start, there would have been nothing left of any of them to free. No Karr to stand down. No congregation to let go. Nothing. The Void doesn't leave anyone behind to be saved later. *You did.*" He held her smooth worn eyes. "You didn't keep them whole for nothing. You kept them whole long enough that someone could come, at last, and give them a real ending. That's not the same as having been right — I still can't tell you that you were right. But it wasn't *nothing*, and it wasn't only cruelty, and you are allowed to lift the seal now without it meaning that you failed. Because the keeping bought the one thing the Void would never in an eternity have allowed: this. Me. Here. Able to let them go gently, one at a time, instead of having them dissolved screaming into nothing an age ago."

Maren was utterly still.

"You're saying," she said, slowly, as though the words were stones she was setting one at a time across deep water, "that I don't have to have been *right.* That I only have to have — bought the time. That the keeping was never meant to be the ending. That it was only ever meant to last until—"

"Until someone could come who belonged to it," Ren said, "and carry it out properly. Yes. You were never the one who was supposed to end it, Maren. That was never your function. Your function was to *decide* — and the thing you decided was to hold the door shut against the Void until the right hands came. And you did that. For an age. Alone. At the bottom. You held the door. And the right hands are here now — not because you were wrong to hold, but because the holding *worked*, and the thing it was holding the door for has finally, finally arrived." He took a breath. "You're allowed to have failed at the thing you thought you were doing — saving them forever — and to have succeeded, completely, at the thing you were actually doing, which was keeping them until I came. Those can both be true. They are both true. You can let the seal fall now. You held the door. You can let it go."

And something in the woman who had sat an age at the bottom of her one choice — something that had been clenched shut since the hour she raised the seal — began, very slowly, to ease.

And then she did the thing he had not braced for, because no one he had freed had ever done it: she turned it back on him.

"You came down carrying something new," Maren said. Her smooth worn eyes had not left his face, and they had read it the way the very tired read the very tired — fluently, without effort, because there is nothing left between them to obscure the view. "I felt it on the stair, before you reached the stone. You let something loose in the Archive, and it took something from you, and you arrived in front of me with a hole in you that wasn't there at the wall or the ward. A fresh one. Still bleeding." She tilted her head a fraction toward the edge of the light, where the tread had stopped, where the calm emptied thing waited and did not cross. "That. Whatever that is. It cost you, on the way down to me. Didn't it."

Ren's throat closed. He had been so fixed on her, on finding her third thing, that he had not noticed her finding his. "It took the boy," he heard himself say. The words came out cracked. "Karr's son. I carried him from the wall. I promised — I told Karr I'd carry him out, I have a rule, I don't—" He stopped. He could not finish it. The hole where the boy had been was too new. "I was proud. I'd won three times and I stopped counting the room, and there was a thing I should have seen and I didn't see it because I'd already decided what I was, and it cost the one I most—" His voice gave out.

"Yes," Maren said, very gently, and it was the gentleness of someone who has stood at the bottom of exactly this for an age. "And now you are going to have to do the thing you just told me to do. Listen to your own voice, living one. You said it to me and it's true and it is just as true turned around, and that is how I know you weren't lying to comfort me — because it indicts you in the same breath. You failed. You failed at the thing you thought you were doing: carrying them all out. The boy is gone, and that is real, and no one gets to tell you it isn't. *And.*" She let the word sit. "And you did not fail at the thing you were actually doing, which was coming down through a dead world giving its trapped souls the first true endings any of them have had in an age. Karr stood down because of you. His boy got to be held by his father before the dark took him — got that, first, because of you, and the dark cannot unmake the having-been-held, only the boy. You are allowed to have failed at the impossible thing and to have done the real thing anyway. You told me I could hold both. Hold both." The faintest, worn ghost of something crossed her face — not a smile, but its grandmother. "You came to give me permission to fail. I think you did not know you were going to need it back."

Ren knelt on the stone with the hole in his chest and the dead woman's worn kindness on him, and understood, all at once, that the thing he had been so proud of being good at — the giving of the third thing — had just been done *to him*, by someone he came to save, and that it was the first time anyone in the dead world had given him anything at all. He had been the one who freed. He had never once been freed. And it undid him in a way none of his own victories had, because it was the thing his oldest hunger had wanted since he woke nameless under the bruise: to be seen, all the way down, by another person, and held there, and not let go.

"There it is," Iris breathed, almost too soft to hear. "Permission to fail. You found it — and she gave it back to you, which I did not arrange and could not have, and which is the only reason you are going to survive what's still ahead. Not absolution — neither of you will ever have that, none of us who decide ever do, that's the price of the function. But permission to set it down anyway. That's the rule you couldn't make in advance, Ren — the one you couldn't carve before you got here, because it can only be found in front of the person who needs it, and it turns out you were one of the people who needed it. *To choose an ending is not to lose.*" A pause, and then, gently, urgently: "But don't let her lift it yet. There's a thing she has to know before that seal comes down — and a thing *you* have to know — because when it falls, everything happens at once, and you need to understand the choice you're actually making before it's on top of you. And there's the matter of the thing waiting at the edge of the light, which the falling seal is going to change. Maren. Hold the door one moment longer. You've held it an age; you can hold it one more breath. There's a god up here who needs, at last, to tell the living boy the truth — and it cannot wait past the seal."

Maren looked at Ren, and at the air where a voice was coming from, and inclined her tired head — and held, one more moment, the door she had held for an age.

And Iris began, at last, to tell Ren what she wanted.
