# Ch13 · Why the Last Native Lived

"Tell me what you did," Ren said. "All of it. I think I've worked out the shape of it, on the way down. But I want it from you, in your own words. Not my guess."

Maren looked at him with her tired, worn, perfectly smooth eyes, and for the first time something moved in her face — not hope, she was an age past hope, but a kind of grim relief at being asked, at last, to say the thing aloud after so long holding it alone in the dark.

"You know we lost," she said. "The war. The thing that came down out of the sky. You'll have seen the Wall on your way in — the marks on it." Her folded hands did not move, but something behind her eyes flinched at the memory of those marks. "We held longer than anyone had any right to hold. Karr held longest of all — I expect you met him first; he was always first, always the outermost. But we lost, the way everything that meets that thing loses, and at the very end there was only this city left, and only hours left to it, and the Void was coming *in.* Not to kill us. I want you to understand that, because it's the whole hinge of everything I did. Killing would have been mercy. It was coming in to *assimilate* us — to take the city, and every soul in it, and unmake us, slowly, into more of itself. Our deaths stretched out into an eternity of being digested. No end to it. No rest in it. Just endless, conscious conversion into nothing." Her hands tightened, once. "I was the Crown. The last of the five with a function still to perform. The Shield holds, the Hearth heals, the Flame keeps hope, the Memory keeps the record — and the Crown *decides.* That was mine. The deciding. And I had one working left in me, one last great making — a seal. I could raise it around the whole city, a wall the Void could not cross. I could save my people from being assimilated."

"But," Ren said softly, because he could feel the *but* coming, could feel the shape of the trap she'd been caught in for an age.

---

"But the seal could not stop the dying," Maren said. "It could only wall it in. The dying was already underway when I raised it — the wounds, the bad air, the failing — and a seal cannot reverse what's already begun, it can only hold the moment still. So I had a choice, in the last hour, and I have had an age since to live inside every corner of it. I could let the Void take us — and we would be unmade, assimilated, every self dissolved into it forever, with no rest at the end because there is no end, only the endless digesting. Or I could raise the seal — and save us from *that*, keep every soul whole and itself and intact and remembered — but trapped. Sealed in the very moment of our dying. Not assimilated. Not at peace either. *Held.* Stopped. An endless half-death in which we keep ourselves but never, ever get to finish being ourselves." She looked at him, and the smooth worn eyes were terrible in their clarity. "I chose the seal. I chose to keep my people as themselves, in an unending half-death, rather than let the Void dissolve them into nothing. I chose all of this. The frozen square you woke in. Karr's drilling. Talwyn's beds. Every stopped step and every gouge in the wall the people were fleeing toward. I did that. On purpose. With my eyes open and my hand steady. Because the only other door led to my whole people becoming *nothing*, and I could not — I *would* not — and so I made them this, instead." Her voice did not break. It was an age past breaking. "And I have wondered, every hour since, whether I chose the crueler thing. An age of being stopped, against an eternity of being dissolved. I still do not know. I have had longer to think about it than your world has existed, and I do not know. That is what you've walked down into, living one. That is the decision at the bottom of your descent. Not a monster in the dark. A woman who had two unbearable doors and time to walk through only one, and walked, and has stood at the bottom of the choice ever since."

"The Rot," Ren said slowly. "The corruption. I thought it was the Void getting in past your seal. Leaking through. But it isn't, is it."

"No," Maren said. "The seal holds. It has always held. The Void has never once gotten through it." A pause. "The Rot is the seal *itself* — doing the only thing a thing can do when it's held against its own ending for too long. You cannot stop a world in the middle of dying and have it stay clean, living one. Life that cannot complete itself — cannot finish dying, cannot finish healing, cannot finish *anything* — sours. It turns. The half-death I sealed them into has been slowly becoming its own kind of unmaking, from the inside, all this time, because a thing forbidden to end will always, in the end, find a way to rot in place. I saved them from being assimilated by the Void from without. And in the saving, I made a smaller, slower, homemade assimilation from within — and it has been doing to us, gently, across an age, the very thing I sealed us away to escape." Her eyes held his. "That is the cruelty I cannot see past. The Void would have unmade them. And so, in the end, am I. I only made it take an age, and called the age *mercy.*"

Ren stood very still in the violet quiet.

"That's why it's spreading to me," he said. "The Rot. My arm. It's not the Void reaching in from outside — it's the seal's own souring, and I walked into the middle of it, alive, and it's trying to fold me into the half-death along with everything else." He looked at his quiet, deadened left arm. "And that's the painless thing. The offer. It was never the Void tempting me — it's *this place,* offering to make me what everyone here already is. Stopped. Kept. Spared the ending. The same offer you took for an entire people, an age ago. The same one Talwyn made me with her warm hands. It's all been the same offer, the whole way down."

"It's all the same offer," Iris agreed, very quietly. "The entire fragment is one choice, made once at the bottom, echoing up through every layer above it. *Be kept, and never end.* Maren was the first ever to make it. You are the last to be asked." A pause. "And now the question you've carried since the first hour, Ren — because you're ready for it, and because she can answer it and I cannot. Ask her. Ask her why you're alive."

---

"Why am I alive," Ren said. "Everyone else in this city died, or was sealed in the dying. I'm the only living thing in the whole fragment. Why me. What am I."

Maren studied him for a long moment, and something in her tired face turned toward pity.

"You don't remember," she said. "I wondered. You came down so steady, so sure of foot — I thought perhaps you'd been spared the knowing of it. You truly don't remember being here. Before."

The violet seemed to go colder around him.

"I was here," Ren said. It was not a question. Something in him had known it since the first hour — since the square had felt less like arrival and more like *return*, since Iris had spoken his name before he gave it to her.

"You were a child," Maren said, gently now. "In the last hour. When I raised the seal, I raised it around everyone inside the walls — every soul, to keep them whole. But a seal takes the living and the dying together and stops them both alike, and there was a child, very small, who was neither quite dying nor quite safe behind the wall — who slipped the very edge of it. Not sealed. Not assimilated. Not dead. Only — caught. In the foundation. In the one place the rot runs thinnest, here at the bottom, near the seed of it. Held in a stasis deeper even than the seal, sleeping, while an age went by in the dark above. I felt you down here all that time — the one warm seed in all the cold — and I could not reach you, and I could not wake you, and I could not let you go. You are the last child of Eldravir, living one. You did not survive the end of your world." Her voice was very quiet. "You slept through it. And someone, very lately, reached all the way down into the bottom of my dead world and woke you, and gave you a name if you'd lost the old one, and set you walking back up through the grave of everyone you were born among, to do the one thing I could not."

The chamber was silent. Ren did not look up, because there was nowhere to look — but he spoke to the voice that had been with him since the first cold breath under the bruised sky.

"You knew," he said. "From the first hour. That's why you used my name before I gave it to you. That's why you chose me — not just a living soul who *could* walk in the stasis, but one who *belonged* to it. The last native. You didn't find your key by luck after five tries. You went looking, specifically, for the one person who was both alive and *from here* — because—" He stopped, the shape of it almost in his hands.

"Because only someone who belonged to it could give it a true ending, instead of merely breaking it," Iris said, and her voice was stripped now of all its dryness, all its careful distance, down to something bare. "Yes. I knew. I have known what you were since before I woke you. I have not lied to you once, Ren — I told you, that first hour, that I didn't know *how* you were alive, and that was true; Maren has only now answered it for the both of us. But I knew *what* you were. And I knew why you, specifically, and not another. And I let you walk up out of your own people's grave without telling you whose grave it was — because if I had told you in the first hour, you would have spent the whole descent drowning in grief instead of learning, and you needed to learn first. You needed to become the man who could carry it before you knew how much there was to carry." A pause. "I'll tell you the rest at the throne. The part about what I want. You've earned the whole of it now. But finish this first — because Maren is still waiting, at the bottom of her choice, for someone to come and tell her whether she damned her people or saved them. And you are the only living child of the people she sealed. There is no one, in any world, with more right to answer her than you."

Ren looked at Maren — at the woman who had sealed his world, and his parents, and the child he himself had been, into an age of stopped half-death rather than let the Void dissolve them into nothing — and understood that the last and hardest thing the whole descent had been walking him toward was not about the dead at all.

It was about whether the choice to keep something past its proper ending could ever, by anyone, be forgiven.
